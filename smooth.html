1 Single-unit vs Multi-unit Smooth Muscle

Compare single-unit and multi-unit smooth muscle regarding their structural arrangement, presence of gap junctions, mode of excitation, and tissue locations.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

  • Single-unit (visceral) smooth muscle: Cells are arranged in sheets or bundles, connected by gap junctions. Exhibits myogenic activity (pacemaker potentials) and responds to stretch. Excitation spreads from cell to cell. Found in gastrointestinal tract, uterus, urinary bladder, ureters.
  • Multi-unit smooth muscle: Cells are discrete and separate, with few or no gap junctions. Excitation is neurogenic (requires nerve stimulation). Each cell responds independently. Found in iris, ciliary muscle, piloerector muscles, and large airways.

2 Neuromuscular Junction of Smooth Muscle

Describe the neuromuscular junction of smooth muscle, contrasting it with the classic motor end plate of skeletal muscle.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Smooth muscle NMJ: Autonomic nerve terminals form a "beaded" network with varicosities that release neurotransmitter into a diffuse junctional cleft (20–100 nm wide). There are no specialized postjunctional folds. Neurotransmitter diffuses to receptors on the smooth muscle membrane, producing a graded junctional potential (EJP or IJP).

Skeletal muscle NMJ: A single motor axon terminal forms a highly specialized motor end plate with deep postjunctional folds. Acetylcholine is released into a narrow cleft (50 nm) and binds to nicotinic receptors, always producing a large end-plate potential that reliably triggers an action potential. Transmission is fast and 1:1.

Contrast: Smooth muscle junction is diffuse, varicose, and graded; skeletal is focal, specialized, and all-or-none.

3 Types of Action Potentials

List three types of action potentials recorded in smooth muscle and state their typical durations.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

  • Spike potential: Duration 10–50 ms; typical of most visceral smooth muscle.
  • Plateau-type potential: Duration 100–150 ms (or longer); found in ureter and some vascular smooth muscle.
  • Pacemaker potential (slow wave): Duration 100–150 ms; found in GI tract and cardiac conducting system; generates rhythmic activity.

4 Ionic Basis of Smooth Muscle AP

Explain the ionic basis of depolarization and repolarization during an action potential in smooth muscle.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Depolarization: Caused primarily by the influx of Ca²⁺ ions through voltage-gated L-type Ca²⁺ channels (slow calcium channels). Na⁺ influx may contribute in some types.

Repolarization: Caused by the efflux of K⁺ ions through voltage-gated K⁺ channels and the closure/inactivation of Ca²⁺ channels. The relatively slow kinetics of Ca²⁺ channels account for the longer duration of the smooth muscle action potential compared to nerve or skeletal muscle.

5 Structural Organization of Contractile Proteins

Describe the structural organization of actin, myosin, and dense bodies within a smooth muscle fiber.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Smooth muscle lacks sarcomeres and Z-lines. Instead:

  • Actin thin filaments: Attach to dense bodies in the cytoplasm and to dense plaques on the inner surface of the plasma membrane.
  • Myosin thick filaments: Longer than in skeletal muscle, with heads along their length. They are arranged in a staggered, diagonal manner between actin filaments.
  • Dense bodies: Electron-dense structures functionally equivalent to Z-lines. They anchor actin filaments and are linked by intermediate filaments (desmin, vimentin) to form a cytoskeletal network.
  • Arrangement: The diagonal arrangement of filaments allows the smooth muscle cell to shorten greatly (up to 80% of resting length) during contraction, unlike skeletal muscle.

6 Excitation-Contraction Coupling

Outline the sequential step-by-step molecular mechanism of excitation-contraction coupling in smooth muscle from Ca²⁺ entry to cross-bridge sliding.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

  1. Depolarization or receptor activation opens voltage-gated or ligand-gated Ca²⁺ channels.
  2. Ca²⁺ enters the cytosol from the extracellular fluid and/or is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (via IP₃ receptors).
  3. Ca²⁺ binds to calmodulin (CaM).
  4. The Ca²⁺–CaM complex activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK).
  5. MLCK phosphorylates the regulatory light chain of myosin.
  6. Phosphorylated myosin heads can now bind to actin and cycle cross-bridges (using ATP), producing contraction.

7 Latch Phenomenon

Define the Latch Phenomenon and explain its physiological advantage in maintaining smooth muscle tone.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Latch phenomenon: A state in which dephosphorylated myosin heads remain attached to actin for prolonged periods, maintaining force with very low ATP consumption. It occurs when MLCK activity decreases and myosin light chain phosphatase (MLP) removes phosphate from myosin heads, but the heads do not detach immediately.

Physiological advantage: Allows smooth muscle to maintain tonic contraction (e.g., in blood vessels, sphincters, airways) for long periods with minimal energy expenditure. This is crucial for organs that must sustain tone continuously, like vascular smooth muscle maintaining blood pressure.

8 Smooth Muscle Relaxation

Explain how smooth muscle relaxation is achieved at the molecular and ion-transport levels.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Relaxation requires:

  • Removal of cytosolic Ca²⁺: Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA) and extruded from the cell by Ca²⁺-ATPase and Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger.
  • Decrease in Ca²⁺–CaM complex → MLCK becomes inactive.
  • Myosin light chain phosphatase (MLP) dephosphorylates the myosin regulatory light chain.
  • Dephosphorylated myosin heads detach from actin → relaxation.

Additionally, β-adrenergic stimulation via cAMP/PKA can phosphorylate MLCK (reducing its activity) and activate MLP, promoting relaxation.

9 EJP vs IJP

Differentiate between Excitatory Junctional Potential (EJP) and Inhibitory Junctional Potential (IJP) in multi-unit smooth muscle.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

  • Excitatory Junctional Potential (EJP): A graded depolarization of the smooth muscle membrane caused by excitatory neurotransmitter (e.g., acetylcholine, norepinephrine). It increases the probability of action potential firing and contraction. It is non-propagated and its amplitude depends on the strength of nerve stimulation.
  • Inhibitory Junctional Potential (IJP): A graded hyperpolarization caused by inhibitory neurotransmitter (e.g., norepinephrine via β-receptors, nitric oxide). It decreases the probability of action potential firing and promotes relaxation.

Both are local, graded potentials that summate and do not obey the all-or-none law.

10 Functions of Smooth Muscle

State five physiological functions of smooth muscle in human organ systems.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

  • Gastrointestinal tract: Peristalsis and mixing movements for digestion and absorption.
  • Blood vessels: Regulation of vascular tone and blood pressure (vasoconstriction/vasodilation).
  • Respiratory system: Control of airway diameter (bronchoconstriction/bronchodilation).
  • Urinary system: Propulsion of urine (ureters) and bladder emptying (detrusor muscle).
  • Reproductive system: Uterine contractions during labor, and erection/ejaculation in males.

1 Calcium Channel Blockers & Vasodilation

A patient is prescribed a calcium channel blocker (e.g., verapamil) for hypertension. Based on smooth muscle excitation-contraction mechanisms, explain how blocking voltage-gated L-type Ca²⁺ channels causes vasodilation in arterial smooth muscle.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Calcium channel blockers inhibit L-type voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels in arterial smooth muscle. This reduces Ca²⁺ influx into the cytosol during depolarization. Lower cytosolic Ca²⁺ → less Ca²⁺–calmodulin complex → reduced activation of MLCK → less phosphorylation of myosin light chains → decreased cross-bridge cycling → relaxation of vascular smooth muscle → vasodilation → decreased peripheral resistance → lower blood pressure.

2 Slow Waves & Peristalsis

During gastrointestinal endoscopy, slow electrical waves are recorded from the stomach muscularis externa. Explain how slow wave potentials give rise to spike action potentials and subsequent intestinal peristalsis.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Slow waves are spontaneous, rhythmic fluctuations in the resting membrane potential of GI smooth muscle (interstitial cells of Cajal act as pacemakers). They are not action potentials but graded depolarizations. When a slow wave reaches a threshold (about −40 mV), voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open, generating one or more spike action potentials. These spikes cause Ca²⁺ influx → contraction. The frequency and amplitude of spikes determine the strength and frequency of contractions. Peristalsis is a coordinated wave of contraction and relaxation driven by these electrical events, propelling contents along the GI tract.

3 Uterine Gap Junctions & Labor

During pregnancy, high levels of progesterone maintain uterine quiescence, whereas near labor, hormonal changes induce high-density gap junction formation and syncytial contraction. Explain the physiological role of gap junctions in converting uterine smooth muscle behavior during labor.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

During pregnancy, progesterone suppresses gap junction formation, keeping uterine smooth muscle cells relatively independent and quiescent (preventing premature labor). Near term, estrogen rises and progesterone falls, stimulating the formation of gap junctions (connexin-43) between uterine smooth muscle cells. Gap junctions provide low-resistance electrical coupling, allowing action potentials and ions to spread rapidly from cell to cell. This converts the uterus into a functional syncytium, enabling coordinated, powerful contractions during labor. The increased coupling also allows oxytocin to act more effectively, further enhancing contractions.

4 Myosin Light Chain Phosphatase Inhibition

A pharmacological drug inhibits the enzyme Myosin Light Chain Phosphatase (MLP) in vascular smooth muscle. Predict the effect of this drug on vascular tone and explain the underlying molecular mechanism.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Effect: Inhibition of MLP would prolong and enhance vascular smooth muscle contraction, leading to increased vascular tone (vasoconstriction) and elevated blood pressure.

Mechanism: Normally, MLP dephosphorylates myosin light chains, leading to relaxation. If MLP is inhibited, phosphorylated myosin light chains accumulate, MLCK activity (even at baseline) maintains phosphorylation, and cross-bridge cycling continues. The muscle cannot relax properly. This would increase peripheral resistance and potentially cause hypertension. Such drugs could be dangerous, but understanding this mechanism is important for developing therapies for conditions like vasospasm.

5 Energy Expenditure: Smooth vs Skeletal Muscle

Compare the energy expenditure (ATP consumption) of smooth muscle maintaining tonic vascular resistance versus skeletal muscle performing continuous isometric contraction, referencing the Latch mechanism.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Smooth muscle (vascular tone): Utilizes the latch mechanism, where dephosphorylated myosin heads remain attached to actin for long periods without cycling. This maintains force with very low ATP consumption (up to 300 times less than skeletal muscle). This allows blood vessels to maintain tonic contraction continuously without fatigue or excessive energy use.

Skeletal muscle (isometric contraction): Cross-bridge cycling continues, with each cycle consuming ATP (for myosin ATPase and Ca²⁺ pumping). Even during isometric contraction, ATP is consumed to maintain tension. Skeletal muscle cannot sustain contraction indefinitely; it fatigues due to ATP depletion, lactic acid accumulation, and other factors.

Conclusion: Smooth muscle is far more energy-efficient for maintaining prolonged tone, thanks to the latch phenomenon. Skeletal muscle is designed for rapid, powerful, but short-lived contractions.
