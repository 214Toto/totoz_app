1 Chemical Synapse Anatomy

Elucidate the anatomical and functional components of a chemical synapse with a clearly labelled diagram.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Components: (1) Presynaptic axon terminal (bouton) containing synaptic vesicles with neurotransmitter, mitochondria, and voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels. (2) Synaptic cleft (20–40 nm) containing extracellular matrix and degrading enzymes (e.g., AChE). (3) Postsynaptic membrane with receptor proteins (ligand-gated ion channels) and postsynaptic density. (4) Diagram should label: axon terminal, synaptic vesicles, Ca²⁺ channels, synaptic cleft, postsynaptic receptors, and mitochondria.

Functional: Action potential depolarizes terminal → opens Ca²⁺ channels → Ca²⁺ influx → vesicle fusion → neurotransmitter release → diffusion across cleft → binding to postsynaptic receptors → ion channel opening → postsynaptic potential.

2 Synapse Classification

Classify synapses based on their anatomical locations, giving FIVE distinct structural types.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

  • Axo-dendritic: Axon terminal → dendrite; most common in CNS.
  • Axo-somatic: Axon terminal → cell body (soma).
  • Axo-axonic: Axon terminal → another axon terminal; mediates presynaptic inhibition/facilitation.
  • Dendro-dendritic: Dendrite → dendrite; found in olfactory bulb and retina.
  • Somato-somatic: Soma → soma; rare, seen in some invertebrate systems.

3 EPSP vs IPSP

Compare and contrast Excitatory Postsynaptic Potentials (EPSP) and Inhibitory Postsynaptic Potentials (IPSP) in terms of ionic mechanisms, effect on membrane potential, and compliance with the All-or-None law.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

EPSP: Caused by opening of ligand-gated Na⁺ (and K⁺) channels → Na⁺ influx → partial depolarization (e.g., −70 mV → −60 mV). IPSP: Caused by opening of Cl⁻ channels (Cl⁻ influx) or K⁺ channels (K⁺ efflux) → hyperpolarization (e.g., −70 mV → −80 mV).

Effect: EPSP brings membrane closer to threshold → excitatory. IPSP moves membrane away from threshold → inhibitory.

All-or-None: Neither EPSP nor IPSP obey the All-or-None law. They are graded potentials — their amplitude varies with stimulus strength and they can summate (temporal/spatial). Action potentials, in contrast, are all-or-none.

4 Convergence & Divergence

Explain the concepts of Convergence and Divergence in neuronal processing networks and write a note on their physiological significance.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Convergence: Multiple presynaptic neurons synapse onto a single postsynaptic neuron. Allows summation of signals from different sources → integration and decision-making. Divergence: A single presynaptic neuron synapses onto multiple postsynaptic neurons. Allows amplification and distribution of a signal to many targets.

Significance: Convergence enables complex integration (e.g., pain perception, memory). Divergence enables widespread coordinated responses (e.g., arousal, motor programs). Both are fundamental to neural network plasticity and information processing.

5 NE & ACh Pathways

Describe the metabolic pathways involved in the synthesis, release, and termination of action for Norepinephrine (NE) and Acetylcholine (ACh).

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

ACh Synthesis: Choline + Acetyl-CoA → (Choline Acetyltransferase, ChAT) → ACh. Release: Ca²⁺-dependent vesicle fusion. Termination: Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) in cleft hydrolyzes ACh → choline + acetate; choline reuptaken into presynaptic terminal.

NE Synthesis: Tyrosine → (Tyrosine hydroxylase) → DOPA → (Dopa decarboxylase) → Dopamine → (Dopamine β-hydroxylase) → NE. Release: Ca²⁺-dependent. Termination: (1) Reuptake into presynaptic terminal (NET) → intracellular degradation by MAO; (2) Extraneuronal uptake → COMT degradation; (3) Diffusion.

6 Presynaptic Inhibition

Explain the mechanisms of Presynaptic Inhibition via GABA_A and GABA_B receptors.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

GABA_A: Ligand-gated Cl⁻ channel. Activation → Cl⁻ influx into presynaptic terminal → slight hyperpolarization → reduces Ca²⁺ influx during action potential → less neurotransmitter release.

GABA_B: Metabotropic G-protein-coupled receptor. Activation → Gβγ subunits directly inhibit voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (decreased Ca²⁺ influx) and open K⁺ channels (hyperpolarization). Both effects reduce neurotransmitter release from the excitatory terminal.

7 Synaptic Delay

Explain the physiological basis of Synaptic Delay and describe how total synaptic delay can be utilized to determine the number of interneurons in a reflex arc.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Basis: Synaptic delay (~0.5 ms) is the time taken for: Ca²⁺ influx → vesicle fusion → neurotransmitter release → diffusion → receptor binding → ion channel opening. It reflects the sequential steps of chemical transmission.

Determining interneurons: Total reflex latency = conduction time along afferent + efferent fibers + (number of synapses × 0.5 ms). By measuring total latency and subtracting known conduction times, the number of central synapses (and thus interneurons) can be estimated. A monosynaptic reflex (e.g., knee jerk) has ~1 synapse; polysynaptic reflexes have more.

8 Long-Term Potentiation

Define Long-Term Potentiation (LTP). State the main neurotransmitter involved and explain its physiological significance in brain function.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

LTP: A persistent increase in synaptic strength following high-frequency stimulation of a synapse. Main neurotransmitter: Glutamate (acting on AMPA and NMDA receptors). Significance: Cellular basis for learning and memory; involves Ca²⁺ influx through NMDA receptors, activation of CaMKII, and increased AMPA receptor insertion. Found in hippocampus and cortex.

9 Local Synaptic Potentials

Explain the bioelectric nature of local synaptic potentials (EPSP and IPSP) and highlight TWO ways in which they differ fundamentally from action potentials.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Nature: EPSPs/IPSPs are graded potentials — their amplitude depends on stimulus strength, they decrement with distance, and they can summate (temporal and spatial). They are generated by ligand-gated ion channels.

Differences from action potentials: (1) Graded vs All-or-None — EPSP/IPSP amplitude varies; AP is all-or-none. (2) Summation — EPSPs/IPSPs can summate; APs cannot. (3) Ionic basis — EPSP/IPSP use ligand-gated channels; AP uses voltage-gated Na⁺/K⁺ channels.

10 One-Way Conduction

Explain why synaptic transmission is strictly ONE-WAY (unidirectional) across a chemical synapse.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Chemical synapses are unidirectional because: (1) Neurotransmitter vesicles are located exclusively in the presynaptic terminal; (2) Receptors for the neurotransmitter are located exclusively on the postsynaptic membrane; (3) The presynaptic membrane lacks receptors for its own neurotransmitter (except autoreceptors for modulation). Therefore, transmission can only proceed from presynaptic → postsynaptic.

11 Acidosis & Hypoxia

Describe the effect of Acidosis and Hypoxia on synaptic transmission and neuronal function.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Acidosis: Decreases neuronal excitability by reducing Na⁺ channel conductance and increasing K⁺ conductance → hyperpolarization. Can depress synaptic transmission and cause confusion, coma.

Hypoxia: Reduces ATP production → impairs Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase → loss of resting potential, impaired neurotransmitter synthesis and vesicle transport → synaptic failure, neuronal death. Severe hypoxia leads to excitotoxicity (excessive glutamate release).

12 GABA_A vs GABA_B

Differentiate between the mechanism of action of GABA_A receptors and GABA_B receptors in producing presynaptic inhibition.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

GABA_A: Ionotropic receptor — ligand-gated Cl⁻ channel. Activation → Cl⁻ influx → slight hyperpolarization of presynaptic terminal → reduces Ca²⁺ influx → decreased neurotransmitter release. Fast, short-lasting.

GABA_B: Metabotropic receptor — G-protein coupled. Activation → Gβγ subunits directly inhibit voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (reduced Ca²⁺ entry) and open K⁺ channels (hyperpolarization). Slower, longer-lasting, and more powerful inhibition of release.

13 ACh Synthesis & Recycling

Write short notes on the synthesis and recycling process of Acetylcholine (ACh) at a cholinergic synapse with the help of a flow diagram.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Synthesis: Choline + Acetyl-CoA → (ChAT) → ACh + CoA. Occurs in presynaptic terminal.

Storage: ACh packaged into synaptic vesicles by VAChT.

Release: Ca²⁺-dependent exocytosis.

Termination: AChE in synaptic cleft hydrolyzes ACh → choline + acetate. Choline is reuptaken by high-affinity choline transporter (CHT1) into presynaptic terminal for resynthesis.

Flow: Choline → (CHT1) → Terminal → + Acetyl-CoA → (ChAT) → ACh → Vesicle → Release → Cleft → (AChE) → Choline + Acetate → Choline reuptake.

14 Subliminal Fringe

Explain the term "Subliminal Fringe" in synaptic physiology and its role in central facilitation.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Subliminal fringe: A population of neurons that receive subthreshold excitatory input — they are partially depolarized but do not fire. They are "primed" and lie just below threshold.

Role in central facilitation: If additional excitatory input arrives (from another source or temporal summation), these neurons can fire more readily. This increases the responsiveness of the pool and contributes to phenomena like reinforcement, learning, and increased reflex gain.

1 Alkalosis & Neuromuscular Hyperexcitability

A 34-year-old patient is admitted with severe neuromuscular hyperexcitability and spontaneous muscle spasms (tetany). ABG analysis reveals hyperventilation-induced systemic alkalosis. Explain from a neurophysiological perspective how changes in extracellular pH affect synaptic transmission and neuronal excitability.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Alkalosis → increased neuronal excitability: Elevated pH increases the permeability of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels (reduces threshold) and decreases K⁺ conductance. This makes neurons more excitable. Additionally, alkalosis reduces ionized calcium (Ca²⁺ binds to albumin more avidly at higher pH), leading to hypocalcemia. Low extracellular Ca²⁺ further increases Na⁺ channel permeability (Ca²⁺ normally stabilizes Na⁺ channels). The combined effect: spontaneous action potentials → tetany, muscle spasms, and even seizures. Synaptic transmission is enhanced because more Ca²⁺ enters presynaptic terminals (due to altered channel gating), increasing neurotransmitter release.

2 Temporal vs Spatial Summation

A neuroscientist applies a series of high-frequency subthreshold electrical impulses to a single presynaptic axon entering a spinal motor neuron, resulting in a postsynaptic action potential. In a second setup, subthreshold stimuli are delivered concurrently through three distinct presynaptic inputs terminating on different dendrites of the same motor neuron. Explain how the postsynaptic cell reaches threshold in each scenario, detailing the concepts of Temporal Summation and Spatial Summation (with needed diagrams labelled).

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Temporal Summation: High-frequency impulses from ONE presynaptic terminal arrive in quick succession. Each EPSP is small and decays, but because they occur close in time, the depolarizations add together. If the summed depolarization reaches threshold, an action potential fires. Diagram: single input → multiple closely spaced EPSPs → summation → threshold.

Spatial Summation: Multiple presynaptic inputs (three different terminals) fire simultaneously. Each produces a small EPSP at different dendritic locations. These EPSPs spread electronically to the axon hillock and summate. If the combined depolarization reaches threshold, an action potential fires. Diagram: three separate inputs → simultaneous EPSPs → summation at trigger zone → threshold.

Key distinction: temporal = same synapse, repeated over time; spatial = different synapses, simultaneous.

3 Renshaw Cell Feedback Inhibition

A patient presents with hypertonia and muscle spasms following a spinal cord injury. Examination demonstrates exaggerated stretch reflexes due to impaired negative feedback regulation on alpha motor neurons. Describe the feedback inhibition circuit involving Renshaw cells in the anterior horn of the spinal cord, detailing the neurotransmitters involved and how this mechanism prevents runaway excitation.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Circuit: Alpha motor neuron (anterior horn) → sends recurrent collateral axon → synapses on Renshaw cell (inhibitory interneuron). Renshaw cell → releases glycine → synapses back onto the alpha motor neuron (and also on Ia inhibitory interneurons). This is a negative feedback loop.

Neurotransmitters: Acetylcholine (from motor neuron collateral → Renshaw cell), Glycine (from Renshaw cell → motor neuron).

Function: When the motor neuron fires excessively, the collateral activates the Renshaw cell, which inhibits the motor neuron, preventing runaway excitation and stabilizing firing rates. Loss of this inhibition (e.g., spinal cord injury) causes hypertonia and exaggerated reflexes.

4 Reciprocal Inhibition in Patellar Reflex

During a patellar tendon reflex test, tapping the patellar ligament causes rapid contraction of the quadriceps (protagonist muscle) while the hamstrings (antagonist muscle) simultaneously relax. Elucidate the neural circuit responsible for Reciprocal Inhibition, highlighting the role of the dorsal root ganglion, motor neurons, and glycine-releasing interneurons.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Circuit: Tapping patellar ligament stretches quadriceps → muscle spindle afferents (Ia fibers) fire → cell bodies in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) → afferents enter spinal cord → (1) directly synapse on alpha motor neurons to quadriceps (monosynaptic) → contraction; (2) collateral branch synapses on Ia inhibitory interneuron → this interneuron releases glycine → synapses on alpha motor neurons to hamstrings (antagonist) → hyperpolarization/inhibition → relaxation.

Roles: DRG contains cell bodies of sensory afferents. Motor neurons (alpha) drive muscle contraction. Glycine-releasing interneuron mediates inhibition of the antagonist motor neuron. This reciprocal inhibition prevents co-contraction and allows smooth, coordinated movement.
