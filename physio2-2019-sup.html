Section B – True or False 10 questions

Indicate whether each statement is True or False, then click Show Answer for the correct response and justification.

Statement 1 – Plasmin
"Plasmin digests fibrinogen into thrombin."
False. Plasmin is a proteolytic enzyme formed from plasminogen. It digests fibrin and fibrinogen, but it does NOT convert fibrinogen into thrombin. Thrombin is formed from prothrombin by the action of factor Xa. Plasmin's role is to break down clots (fibrinolysis).
Statement 2 – Iron Metabolism
"Most of the iron in the body is contained in ferritin."
False. Most of the iron in the body (about 65–70%) is contained in haemoglobin. Ferritin is the main storage form of iron in tissues, but it contains only a small fraction of total body iron (about 25%). The body contains about 4–5 g of iron in total.
Statement 3 – Blood Coagulation
"Blood coagulation through the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways results in activation of factor X."
True. Both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways converge on the activation of factor X to factor Xa. Factor Xa then combines with factor V, phospholipid, and calcium to form the prothrombin activator complex, which converts prothrombin to thrombin.
Statement 4 – Iron Absorption
"Ferrous iron is better absorbed than ferric iron."
True. Ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) is more soluble and better absorbed than ferric iron (Fe³⁺). Ferric iron must be reduced to ferrous iron (by vitamin C or gastric acid) before it can be absorbed in the duodenum and proximal jejunum.
Statement 5 – Rhesus Blood Group
"The first pregnancy is never affected by Rhesus disease."
True (usually). The first pregnancy is typically unaffected because sensitization of the Rh‑negative mother occurs during delivery, when fetal Rh‑positive red cells enter the maternal circulation. The mother develops anti‑Rh antibodies after the first delivery, which can affect subsequent pregnancies. However, if the mother was previously sensitized (e.g., by transfusion or abortion), the first pregnancy can be affected.
Statement 6 – Functional Residual Capacity
"Functional residual capacity falls with general anaesthesia."
True. General anaesthesia causes a decrease in functional residual capacity (FRC) due to relaxation of the diaphragm and chest wall muscles, changes in posture (supine), and altered lung mechanics. This can lead to atelectasis and impaired gas exchange.
Statement 7 – Respiratory Dead Space
"The respiratory dead space decreases during a deep inspiration."
False. The anatomical dead space remains relatively constant (about 150 mL) because it is determined by the volume of the conducting airways. During deep inspiration, the airways may distend slightly, but the dead space does not decrease significantly. In fact, it may increase slightly.
Statement 8 – Vital Capacity
"Vital capacity is greater in men than in women of the same age and height."
True. Vital capacity is greater in men than in women of the same age and height due to larger lung volumes and greater muscle mass in men. It also decreases with age and is affected by body size, posture, and respiratory muscle strength.
Statement 9 – Emphysema
"Loss of pulmonary elastic tissue in emphysema reduces the percentage of vital capacity expired in one second."
True. Emphysema causes loss of elastic recoil, which reduces the driving pressure for expiration. This leads to air trapping and a decreased FEV₁/FVC ratio (the percentage of vital capacity expired in one second). This is a hallmark of obstructive lung disease.
Statement 10 – Vital Capacity
"Vital capacity is higher in obese individuals compared to tall slim individuals."
False. Vital capacity is lower in obese individuals due to increased abdominal pressure, reduced chest wall compliance, and decreased lung volumes. Tall slim individuals have larger lungs and greater vital capacity. Obesity restricts diaphragm movement and reduces respiratory reserve.

Question 1 – Glucose Transport (5 marks)
Add the membrane transporters and other components/metabolites to this diagram needed to show how glucose is transported from the intestinal lumen through endothelial cells to the circulatory system. Explain the roles of active and passive transport steps in the transport process.
Model answer:
Diagram should include:
• Apical membrane: SGLT1 (Na⁺‑glucose cotransporter) — secondary active transport. Na⁺ moves down its electrochemical gradient (maintained by Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase) and glucose moves against its concentration gradient into the cell.
• Basolateral membrane: GLUT2 (facilitated diffusion) — glucose moves down its concentration gradient into the interstitial fluid and then into the blood.
• Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase on the basolateral membrane — primary active transport, pumps Na⁺ out and K⁺ in, maintaining the Na⁺ gradient necessary for SGLT1.

Roles:
• Active transport (SGLT1 + Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase): enables glucose uptake against its concentration gradient from the lumen.
• Passive transport (GLUT2): allows glucose to exit the cell down its concentration gradient into the blood.

Question 3 – Physiologic Dead Space (7 marks)
Use the following information to answer the questions:
Breathing frequency = 12/min; Tidal volume = 500 mL; PaCO₂ = 40 mmHg; PaO₂ = 100 mmHg; PECO₂ = 30 mmHg; PICO₂ = 0; VCO₂ = 200 mL/min; VO₂ = 250 mL/min.
a) What is the volume of the physiologic dead space? (3 marks)
Using Bohr's equation: VD = VT × (PaCO₂ − PECO₂) / PaCO₂
VD = 500 × (40 − 30) / 40 = 500 × 10/40 = 500 × 0.25 = 125 mL.
Physiologic dead space ≈ 125 mL.
b) What is the value for minute ventilation? (2 marks)
Minute ventilation (VE) = Breathing frequency × Tidal volume
VE = 12 × 500 = 6000 mL/min = 6.0 L/min.
c) What is the value for alveolar ventilation? (2 marks)
Alveolar ventilation (VA) = (Tidal volume − Dead space) × Breathing frequency
VA = (500 − 125) × 12 = 375 × 12 = 4500 mL/min = 4.5 L/min.
d) What is alveolar PCO₂ (PACO₂)? (2 marks)
PACO₂ = (VCO₂ × K) / VA, where K = 863 (STPD correction).
PACO₂ = (200 × 863) / 4500 = 172600 / 4500 ≈ 38.4 mmHg.
Alternatively, since PaCO₂ ≈ PACO₂ in steady state, PACO₂ ≈ 40 mmHg.
e) What is the value for alveolar PO₂ (PAO₂)? (2 marks)
Using the alveolar gas equation:
PAO₂ = PIO₂ − (PACO₂ / R)
PIO₂ = FiO₂ × (PB − 47) = 0.21 × (760 − 47) = 0.21 × 713 ≈ 150 mmHg.
PAO₂ = 150 − (40 / 0.8) = 150 − 50 = 100 mmHg.
Question 4 – V/Q Ratio (4 marks)
Ventilation to the apex of the lung is 0.8 L/min and ventilation to the base of the lung is 2.2 L/min. Blood flow to the apex is 0.4 L/min and blood flow to the base is 3.2 L/min. What is the V/Q ratio at the apex and the base of the lung, and what effect would you expect any differences to have on PO₂ and PCO₂ in those regions?
Model answer:
Apex: V/Q = 0.8 / 0.4 = 2.0
Base: V/Q = 2.2 / 3.2 ≈ 0.69

At the apex, V/Q is high (2.0), meaning ventilation exceeds perfusion. This leads to a higher alveolar PO₂ (because more O₂ is brought in than is removed by blood) and a lower alveolar PCO₂ (because more CO₂ is blown off than is brought in).
At the base, V/Q is low (0.69), meaning perfusion exceeds ventilation. This leads to a lower alveolar PO₂ and a higher alveolar PCO₂.
This regional difference is normal and reflects gravity‑dependent differences in blood flow and ventilation.
Question 5 – Minimal Air (3 marks)
What is minimal air? And what is its importance?
Model answer:
Minimal air is the small volume of air that remains in the lungs even when the chest is opened and the lungs collapse. It is about 1 litre (or less, ~500–1000 mL) and is trapped in the alveoli and terminal bronchioles.

Importance:
1. It prevents complete collapse of the alveoli, which would require enormous pressures to re‑expand.
2. It is used in forensic medicine (hydrostatic test) to determine whether a baby was born alive or dead — if the lungs float in water, minimal air is present, indicating the baby breathed after birth.
Question 6 – Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn (5 marks)
Describe the hemolytic disease of the newborn.
Model answer:
Hemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN), also called erythroblastosis fetalis, occurs when a Rh‑negative mother carries a Rh‑positive fetus.

Mechanism:
1. During pregnancy or delivery, fetal Rh‑positive red cells leak into the maternal circulation.
2. The mother's immune system recognises the Rh antigen as foreign and produces anti‑Rh antibodies (IgG).
3. In subsequent pregnancies, these IgG antibodies cross the placenta and attack the red cells of a Rh‑positive fetus.
4. This causes haemolysis of fetal red cells, leading to anaemia, jaundice (due to bilirubin release), and possibly hydrops fetalis.

Prevention: Anti‑Rh immunoglobulins (RhoGAM) given to the mother within 72 hours of delivery (or abortion) to prevent sensitization.
Treatment: Exchange transfusion for the affected newborn, or intrauterine transfusion in severe cases.
