Q1
A 64-year-old female presents to OPD with a fungating right breast tumour. The most important investigation for this patient is:
A. Full blood count (FBC) B. Renal function test C. Chest X-ray D. Biopsy of the tumour E. Abdominal ultrasound
Answer: D — Biopsy of the tumour
Why? A fungating breast mass is highly suspicious for malignancy. The definitive diagnosis requires histopathological confirmation via core needle biopsy (or incisional biopsy if ulcerated). FBC, RFTs, CXR, and ultrasound are staging or baseline investigations, not diagnostic.

Key point: Always obtain tissue diagnosis before definitive surgery or systemic therapy.
High-Yield: Triple assessment of a breast lump = clinical examination + imaging (mammogram/US) + tissue sampling (FNA or core biopsy). Core biopsy is preferred for solid masses as it provides architectural information and allows receptor testing (ER/PR/HER2).
Q2
The following are causes of upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding except:
A. Diverticular disease B. Erosive gastric ulceration C. Oesophageal varices D. Gastric tumour E. Duodenal ulcer
Answer: A — Diverticular disease
Why? Upper GI bleeding is defined as bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz (duodenal-jejunal flexure). Causes include peptic ulcer disease (gastric/duodenal), erosive gastritis, oesophageal varices, Mallory-Weiss tears, and upper GI malignancies.

Diverticular disease (e.g., colonic diverticulosis) typically causes lower GI bleeding (haematochezia), not upper GI bleeding.
High-Yield: The most common cause of upper GI bleeding is peptic ulcer disease. The most common cause of lower GI bleeding in adults is diverticular disease. Upper GI bleed presents with haematemesis and/or melaena; lower GI bleed with fresh rectal bleeding.
Q3
Most common cause of intestinal obstruction:
A. Sigmoid volvulus B. Abdominal adhesive disease C. Hernias D. Worm infestation E. Colorectal tumours
Answer: B — Abdominal adhesive disease
Why? In the developed world, adhesions (post-surgical) are the leading cause of small bowel obstruction (SBO). In the developing world, hernias are a very common cause, but globally, adhesions are the most frequent overall.

Key point: Adhesions account for up to 60–70% of SBO cases in patients with prior abdominal surgery.
High-Yield: For large bowel obstruction, the most common cause in the developing world is volvulus (sigmoid), while in the developed world it is colorectal carcinoma. Always consider hernia as a cause of obstruction in resource-limited settings.
Q4
The goals in the treatment of a burns patient include the following except:
A. Ensure survival of a patient B. Prevent burns infection C. Avoid burns healing by scar tissue formation D. Patient is well hydrated E. None of the above
Answer: C — Avoid burns healing by scar tissue formation
Why? Burns healing inevitably involves some degree of scarring, especially in deep partial-thickness and full-thickness burns. The goal is not to avoid scar formation entirely but to minimise scarring through proper wound care, grafting, and rehabilitation. The other options (survival, infection prevention, hydration) are core management goals.
High-Yield: Burns management priorities: ABC (Airway, Breathing, Circulation), fluid resuscitation (Parkland formula), infection control, wound care, and early nutrition. Scarring is managed with pressure garments, silicone sheets, and physiotherapy.
Q5
The following are absorbable sutures except:
A. PDS B. Vicryl C. Chromic catgut D. Polysorb E. Nylon
Answer: E — Nylon
Why? Nylon is a non-absorbable suture material. PDS (polydioxanone), Vicryl (polyglactin 910), chromic catgut, and Polysorb are all absorbable. Nylon is used for skin closure and requires removal.
High-Yield: Absorbable sutures: Vicryl (braided, absorb 60–90 days), Monocryl (monofilament, 90–120 days), PDS (monofilament, 180 days), catgut (fast/chromic). Non-absorbable: nylon, silk, prolene (polypropylene), steel.
Q6
Regarding level of wound contamination, which of the following is the most contaminated?
A. Clean contaminated wound B. Dirty wound
Answer: B — Dirty wound
Why? Surgical wounds are classified as: Clean (no inflammation, no viscus entered), Clean-contaminated (viscus entered under controlled conditions), Contaminated (traumatic wounds, gross spillage), and Dirty/infected (pus, perforated viscus, old traumatic wound). Dirty wounds have the highest infection risk.
High-Yield: Infection rates: Clean ~1–2%, Clean-contaminated ~5–10%, Contaminated ~15–20%, Dirty ~30–40%. Prophylactic antibiotics are indicated for clean-contaminated and contaminated wounds.
Q7
A patient was diagnosed with peritonitis. The best action is:
A. Keep patient nil per oral B. Prepare and book patient for exploratory laparotomy C. Withhold analgesia D. Initiate antibiotics E. Insert a canula, NGT and urinary catheter
Answer: B — Prepare and book patient for exploratory laparotomy
Why? Peritonitis (especially generalized) is a surgical emergency. The definitive management is exploratory laparotomy to identify and treat the source (e.g., perforated ulcer, appendicitis). While antibiotics, IV fluids, and NGT are part of resuscitation, the best action is surgical intervention.
High-Yield: Peritonitis presents with rigid abdomen, rebound tenderness, and fever. Delay in surgery increases mortality. Resuscitation (fluids, antibiotics) should happen concurrently, not instead of surgery.
Q8
The following are risk factors of breast cancer except:
A. Early menarche B. Early menopause C. Nulliparity D. Obesity E. Positive family history of breast cancer
Answer: B — Early menopause
Why? Early menopause (before age 45) is associated with a reduced risk of breast cancer because of less lifetime exposure to oestrogen. Late menopause (after 55) increases risk. Early menarche (<12 years), nulliparity, obesity, and family history are all established risk factors.
High-Yield: Reproductive risk factors relate to cumulative oestrogen exposure: early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, first pregnancy after 30, and hormone replacement therapy all increase risk.
Q9
Indications for thyroidectomy include the following except:
A. Cosmetic reason B. Respiratory distress due to trachea compression C. Pregnancy D. If it is a cancer E. None of the above
Answer: C — Pregnancy
Why? Pregnancy is not an indication for thyroidectomy; in fact, surgery is generally deferred until after delivery unless there is a compelling reason (e.g., rapidly growing cancer). Indications include cosmetic concerns, compressive symptoms (tracheal compression), malignancy, and thyrotoxicosis refractory to medical therapy.
High-Yield: Thyroidectomy indications: compressive symptoms (dysphagia, dyspnoea, hoarseness), malignancy (papillary, follicular, medullary, anaplastic), thyrotoxicosis unresponsive to drugs, and cosmetic large goitres. Pregnancy is a relative contraindication.
Q10
Criteria for admission to intensive care unit include the following except:
A. Burns in special areas B. For intensive monitoring after a major surgery C. Patient who require mechanical ventilation D. Patient with a higher chance of survival E. Critically ill moribund patient
Answer: E — Critically ill moribund patient
Why? ICU admission is generally based on the potential for recovery and the need for intensive monitoring or organ support. A moribund patient (with no realistic chance of survival) is usually not admitted to ICU, as resources may be better directed to those with reversible conditions. The other options are valid ICU admission criteria.
High-Yield: Common ICU admission criteria: haemodynamic instability, respiratory failure requiring ventilation, need for invasive monitoring, post-operative major surgery, and severe burns. Triage should consider futility.
Q11
The following are values of investigations in surgery except:
A. Monitoring response to treatment B. Making a diagnosis C. For baseline data D. For medical legal purpose E. None of the above
Answer: E — None of the above
Why? All the listed options are valid reasons for ordering investigations in surgery: monitoring treatment response, diagnosis, baseline data, and medico-legal documentation. Therefore, none of them is an exception.
High-Yield: Investigations should be justified by their impact on management. Over-investigation increases cost and may cause harm. Always ask: Will this result change my management?
Q12
The most appropriate investigation for a patient suspected to have obstructive jaundice is:
A. Liver function test B. Urinalysis C. Serum bilirubin D. Ultrasound of the biliary tree E. Clotting profile
Answer: D — Ultrasound of the biliary tree
Why? Ultrasound is the first-line imaging modality for suspected obstructive jaundice. It can detect gallstones, biliary duct dilatation, and mass lesions. LFTs and bilirubin support the diagnosis but do not localise the obstruction.
High-Yield: Obstructive jaundice: elevated direct bilirubin, ALP, GGT. Ultrasound is non-invasive and readily available. If ultrasound is inconclusive, MRCP or ERCP may be used.
Q13
Best action to take in a patient with traumatic brain injury GCS=8/15 and also having nasal and ear bleeding include the following except:
A. Initiation of supplemental oxygen B. Prop up patient 30 to 40 degrees C. Initiate broad spectrum IV antibiotics D. Apply a rigid cervical collar E. Insert a nasal gastric tube
Answer: E — Insert a nasal gastric tube
Why? In a patient with suspected basal skull fracture (nasal/ear bleeding), nasogastric tube insertion is contraindicated due to the risk of intracranial placement. Use an orogastric tube instead if needed. The other actions (oxygen, head elevation, antibiotics if open fracture, cervical collar) are appropriate.
High-Yield: Signs of basal skull fracture: raccoon eyes, Battle's sign, CSF rhinorrhoea/otorrhoea, haemotympanum. Avoid nasal intubation/NG tube. Give prophylactic antibiotics for open skull fractures.
Q14
Itching in a patient with obstructive jaundice is relieved by the following drug:
A. Diazepam B. Diltiazem C. Hydrocortisone D. Pethidine E. Cholestyramine
Answer: E — Cholestyramine
Why? Pruritus in obstructive jaundice is caused by bile salt deposition in the skin. Cholestyramine is a bile acid sequestrant that binds bile salts in the gut, reducing their reabsorption and relieving itching. Other options: rifampicin, naltrexone, sertraline.
High-Yield: Cholestyramine is first-line for cholestatic pruritus. Side effects: constipation, bloating, and interference with absorption of fat-soluble vitamins and drugs (e.g., warfarin).
Q15
Regarding toxic goiter, which of the following is not true?
A. Lid lag is a feature B. Hot and moist palms are a feature C. Rapid pulse is a feature D. Exophthalmos is a feature E. None of the above
Answer: E — None of the above
Why? All listed features (lid lag, warm/moist palms, tachycardia, exophthalmos) are typical of thyrotoxicosis (toxic goitre). Therefore, none of them is "not true."
High-Yield: Features of thyrotoxicosis: weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, palpitations, goitre, ophthalmopathy (exophthalmos, lid lag, periorbital oedema). Management: antithyroid drugs, radioactive iodine, or surgery.
Q16
The following are factors that delay wound healing except:
A. Poor blood supply B. Infection C. Poor nutrition D. Poorly controlled diabetes mellitus E. Young age
Answer: E — Young age
Why? Young age is associated with faster and better wound healing. Advanced age delays healing. Poor blood supply, infection, malnutrition, and diabetes are all well-known factors that impair wound healing.
High-Yield: Factors delaying healing: age, malnutrition, ischaemia, infection, diabetes, steroids, smoking, chemotherapy, radiation. Optimise these before elective surgery.
Q17
Ultrasound in a jaundiced patient can demonstrate the following except:
A. Cholangiocarcinoma B. Cholelithiasis C. Intrahepatic biliary dilatation D. Extrahepatic biliary dilatation E. None of the above
Answer: E — None of the above
Why? Ultrasound can detect all of these: cholangiocarcinoma (as a mass or ductal thickening), gallstones, and both intra- and extrahepatic biliary dilatation. Therefore, none is an exception.
High-Yield: Ultrasound is excellent for gallstones and biliary dilatation. It is less sensitive for common bile duct stones (distal). MRCP is superior for choledocholithiasis and biliary tree anatomy.
Q18
A patient who undergoes an elective splenectomy is more susceptible to:
A. Bacteroides infection B. Encapsulated organisms infection C. Gram-positive bacteria D. Gram-negative bacteria E. Streptococci
Answer: B — Encapsulated organisms infection
Why? The spleen is crucial for clearing encapsulated bacteria. Post-splenectomy, patients are at risk of overwhelming sepsis from Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Neisseria meningitidis. Vaccination and prophylactic antibiotics are essential.
High-Yield: Post-splenectomy: vaccinate against pneumococcus, meningococcus, Hib. Lifelong penicillin prophylaxis may be considered. Also at risk of thrombocytosis and malaria severity.
Q19
Which of the following is not associated with paralytic ileus?
A. Electrolyte imbalance B. Mechanical intestinal obstruction
Answer: B — Mechanical intestinal obstruction
Why? Paralytic ileus is a functional obstruction due to cessation of bowel motility without a physical blockage. Mechanical obstruction involves a physical barrier (adhesions, tumour, hernia). Electrolyte imbalance (especially hypokalaemia) is a common cause of ileus.
High-Yield: Paralytic ileus: absent bowel sounds, distension, no flatus. Causes: post-op, electrolytes, drugs (opioids), sepsis, retroperitoneal bleed. Management: NG decompression, IV fluids, correct electrolytes, treat cause.
Q20
Regarding Alvarado score, its main objective is to:
A. Assess if a patient has appendicitis B. To decide whether or not a patient with suspected appendicitis should be admitted or not C. To assess other conditions that could cause right iliac fossa pain D. Admit all patients with right iliac fossa pain, fever or nausea E. None of the above
Answer: B — To decide whether or not a patient with suspected appendicitis should be admitted or not
Why? The Alvarado score (MANTRELS) is a clinical decision tool that stratifies patients with suspected appendicitis into low, intermediate, and high probability, guiding the need for admission, observation, or surgery. It is not diagnostic on its own.
High-Yield: Alvarado score components: Migration of pain to RIF, Anorexia, Nausea/vomiting, Tenderness in RIF, Rebound tenderness, Elevated temperature, Leucocytosis, Shift to left (neutrophilia). Score 1–4: discharge; 5–6: observe; 7–10: operate.
Q21
One best investigation that could be ordered for a patient with suspected intestinal obstruction is:
A. Chest X-ray B. Abdominal ultrasound C. Supine plain abdominal X-ray D. Erect plain abdominal X-ray E. Liver function test
Answer: D — Erect plain abdominal X-ray
Why? The erect abdominal X-ray is the best initial imaging for suspected intestinal obstruction because it can demonstrate air-fluid levels (multiple, stepped) which are characteristic of obstruction. Supine films may show distended loops but lack air-fluid levels.
High-Yield: Erect CXR may show free air under the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum). Supine AXR is useful for volvulus (coffee bean sign) and general bowel gas pattern. CT abdomen is the gold standard for obstruction.
Q22
An X-ray to order for a patient with suspected intestinal obstruction who is unable to stand is:
A. Lateral semi-decubitus X-ray B. Supine X-ray C. Erect X-ray D. Paranasal air sinus X-ray E. None of the above
Answer: A — Lateral semi-decubitus X-ray
Why? When a patient cannot stand, the lateral decubitus view (patient lying on their side) can still demonstrate air-fluid levels. Supine films do not show air-fluid levels well. Erect is ideal but often impossible in severely ill patients.
High-Yield: In the acute setting, if erect is not possible, lateral decubitus is the alternative to look for free air and air-fluid levels. CT is the definitive imaging.
Q23
The main role of a medical doctor in the management of a traumatic brain injury is:
A. To prevent secondary brain injury B. To prevent primary brain injury C. To prop up patient, apply a cervical collar and initiate supplemental oxygen D. To insert nasogastric tube because early feeding is very important E. To quickly admit patient to intensive care unit
Answer: A — To prevent secondary brain injury
Why? Primary brain injury occurs at the time of trauma and cannot be prevented. The doctor's role is to prevent secondary injury by maintaining oxygenation, blood pressure, and cerebral perfusion, and by avoiding hypoxia, hypotension, hypercapnia, and hypoglycaemia.
High-Yield: Secondary brain injury prevention: avoid hypotension (systolic BP < 90), hypoxia (SpO2 < 90%), hypercarbia, hyperthermia, hyperglycaemia. Head elevation 30°, normocapnia, and osmotic therapy if raised ICP.
Q24
The following are risk factors of dry gangrene except:
A. Advancement in age B. Diabetes mellitus C. Hypertension D. Hyperlipidemia E. Hypotension
Answer: E — Hypotension
Why? Dry gangrene is caused by ischaemia (arterial insufficiency). Risk factors include atherosclerosis, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, and ageing. Hypotension is more associated with wet gangrene (due to infection and poor perfusion) or generalised shock, but it is not a classic risk factor for dry gangrene.
High-Yield: Dry gangrene: dry, shrivelled, black tissue, clear demarcation. Wet gangrene: swollen, discoloured, foul-smelling, often due to infection. Treatment of dry gangrene: revascularisation or amputation.
Q25
You receive a patient with severe hypovolaemic shock. The best fluid to administer is:
A. A colloid B. Ringer's lactate C. Normal saline D. A crystalloid
Answer: C — Normal saline (or balanced crystalloid like Ringer's lactate)
Why? In hypovolaemic shock, isotonic crystalloids (normal saline or Ringer's lactate) are the first-line fluids for rapid volume expansion. Normal saline is often preferred for initial resuscitation because it is readily available and compatible with blood products. Ringer's lactate is also acceptable but may cause hyperkalaemia in renal failure. Colloids are not superior and are more expensive.
High-Yield: Hypovolaemic shock: 1–2 L of warmed crystalloid as initial bolus. Blood products are indicated for haemorrhagic shock with ongoing bleeding. Avoid excessive crystalloid (may cause dilutional coagulopathy and oedema).
Q26
The following are part of treatment modalities for a patient with cancer:
A. Surgery B. Chemotherapy C. Analgesia D. Radiotherapy E. All of the above
Answer: E — All of the above
Why? Cancer treatment is multimodal: surgery (curative or palliative), chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and analgesia (palliative care). All are part of comprehensive cancer management.
High-Yield: Multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach is standard. Treatment intent: curative vs palliative. Analgesia follows the WHO ladder (non-opioid → weak opioid → strong opioid).
Q27
Post-operative care of a patient after major surgery includes:
A. Care for anaesthesia B. Monitor vitals C. Always examine the wound D. Ensure intravenous fluids are being administered in a nil per oral patient E. All of the above
Answer: E — All of the above
Why? Post-operative care is comprehensive: anaesthetic recovery, vital sign monitoring, wound inspection, and maintenance of IV fluids until oral intake resumes. All are essential components.
High-Yield: Post-op care: airway, breathing, circulation, pain control, fluid balance, wound care, early mobilisation, DVT prophylaxis, and monitoring for complications (bleeding, infection, ileus).
Q28
Appropriate investigation to order in a patient with dysphagia is:
A. Full blood count B. Barium swallow C. Oesophagoscopy D. Abdominal ultrasound E. Fasting blood sugar
Answer: C — Oesophagoscopy
Why? Dysphagia requires direct visualisation of the oesophagus to rule out malignancy, strictures, or achalasia. Oesophagogastroduodenoscopy (OGD) is the gold standard and allows biopsy. Barium swallow is an alternative if endoscopy is unavailable or for motility disorders.
High-Yield: Dysphagia: "food sticking" — investigate with OGD. If OGD normal, consider oesophageal manometry for achalasia. Alarm features: weight loss, anaemia, vomiting, age >50.
Q29
The following are determinants on when to start oral feeds after a major surgery except:
A. When patient starts passing flatus B. When stoma is active C. When contents of the nasogastric drain change from green to clear contents D. When patient opens bowels E. When patient demands food
Answer: E — When patient demands food
Why? Readiness for oral intake after major surgery is assessed by return of bowel function (flatus, bowel movement), absence of nausea/vomiting, and decreasing NG output. Patient demand alone is not a reliable criterion.
High-Yield: Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols promote early oral feeding (within 24 hours) as tolerated, rather than waiting for flatus. NG tube removal early and early mobilisation improve outcomes.
Q30
The following are the most important factors in the prevention of diabetic foot except:
A. Wearing open shoes B. Avoid hitting feet against objects C. Strict adherence to recommended diet D. Ensure tight serum sugar control E. All of the above
Answer: A — Wearing open shoes
Why? Diabetic foot prevention requires protective footwear (closed, well-fitting shoes) to prevent trauma and ulcers. Open shoes (e.g., sandals) expose the foot to injury and are not recommended. Tight glycaemic control, diet, and avoiding trauma are key preventive measures.
High-Yield: Diabetic foot care: inspect feet daily, moisturise, wear proper footwear, avoid walking barefoot, control blood sugar, and treat neuropathy and vascular disease. Multidisciplinary foot clinic reduces amputations.
Q31
The following are recommended methods for treatment of hernias except:
A. Hernioplasty B. Herniotomy C. Herniorrhaphy D. Laparoscopic hernia repair E. Conservative management
Answer: E — Conservative management
Why? Hernias (except in specific circumstances like small asymptomatic umbilical hernias in infants) are generally treated surgically. Conservative management (watchful waiting) is not recommended for most adult hernias due to the risk of complications (strangulation, incarceration). Surgical options include hernioplasty (mesh), herniotomy (ligation of sac), herniorrhaphy (suture repair), and laparoscopic repair.
High-Yield: Hernia repair: Lichtenstein tension-free mesh repair is the gold standard for inguinal hernia. Laparoscopic repair (TEP/TAPP) is an alternative. Emergency surgery for strangulation.
Q32
A 45-year-old Asian man is brought in with an acute onset of epigastric pain, nausea and severe vomiting. The pain is worse with movement and is only relieved slightly by leaning forward. The patient is an alcoholic and has been admitted to the emergency department on several occasions for alcohol intoxication. On examination the patient is tachycardic, pyrexia and dehydrated. The abdomen is diffusely tender and soft, and bowel sounds are normal. The patient's serum amylase is raised by six times the upper limit of normal. The most likely diagnosis is:
A. Perforated peptic ulcer B. Small bowel obstruction C. Acute cholecystitis D. Acute pancreatitis E. None of the above
Answer: D — Acute pancreatitis
Why? The classic presentation of acute pancreatitis is severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, relieved by leaning forward, with nausea/vomiting. Alcohol is a major cause. A serum amylase >3× the upper limit of normal is highly suggestive. The soft abdomen and normal bowel sounds differentiate from peritonitis.
High-Yield: Acute pancreatitis diagnosis: 2 of 3 — characteristic pain, amylase/lipase >3× normal, and imaging (CT). Management: aggressive IV fluids, analgesia, nil by mouth initially, and early enteral feeding when tolerated. Complications: necrosis, pseudocyst, ARDS.
Q33
From the list of options below, which one is the most unlikely cause of mechanical intestinal obstruction?
A. Faecal impaction B. Caecal volvulus C. Paralytic ileus D. Congenital intestinal atresia E. Crohn's colitis
Answer: C — Paralytic ileus
Why? Paralytic ileus is a functional obstruction, not a mechanical one. All the other options are mechanical causes: faecal impaction, volvulus, atresia, and Crohn's strictures.
High-Yield: Mechanical obstruction: physical barrier. Functional (ileus): no barrier, absent peristalsis. Distinguish clinically: ileus has absent bowel sounds and no colicky pain; mechanical has hyperactive, high-pitched bowel sounds and colicky pain.
Q34
You are asked to give your opinion on an abdominal plain film radiograph of a patient with bowel obstruction. The film shows distended loops of large bowel which form a 'U' shape, giving the appearance of a big coffee bean. Select the most likely reason for the large bowel obstruction:
A. Faecal impaction B. Sigmoid volvulus C. Obstructing carcinoma D. Foreign body E. None of the above
Answer: B — Sigmoid volvulus
Why? The "coffee bean" sign on a plain abdominal X-ray is classic for sigmoid volvulus. A distended loop of sigmoid colon twists on its mesentery, creating a U-shaped, coffee-bean appearance originating from the left lower quadrant.
High-Yield: Sigmoid volvulus: elderly, constipated patients. AXR: coffee bean sign. Management: endoscopic decompression with flatus tube (sigmoidoscopy) followed by elective sigmoid colectomy to prevent recurrence. Caecal volvulus: "embryo" or "comma" sign, requires surgery.
Q35
Which one of the following statements regarding stomas is the most correct?
A. Hartmann's procedure results in a defunctional rectum and loop colostomy B. A mucus fistula functions to decompress residual distal bowel C. An abdominoperineal resection may be reversed by formation of an ileal J-pouch D. Small bowel obstruction is a common early complication and indicates need for revision E. Positioning of the stoma is not important
Answer: B — A mucus fistula functions to decompress residual distal bowel
Why? A mucus fistula is created to drain mucus from the distal bowel segment after procedures like Hartmann's. It decompresses the defunctionalised rectum and allows for easier future reversal. The other options are incorrect: Hartmann's is an end colostomy, not loop; APR is not reversible with a J-pouch (that's for ulcerative colitis); SBO is a late complication; stoma positioning is crucial.
High-Yield: Stoma types: end colostomy (Hartmann's), loop colostomy, ileostomy, urostomy. Stoma care and patient education are vital. Complications: skin irritation, prolapse, retraction, obstruction, parastomal hernia.
Q36
An 85-year-old male patient with a history of chronic constipation presents with acute severe colicky abdominal pain and absolute constipation. Plain abdominal film shows a grossly dilated oval of large bowel arising from the left lower quadrant. A diagnosis of sigmoid volvulus is made. The next step in management is:
A. Laparotomy B. Sigmoidoscopy with flatus tube insertion C. Sigmoid colectomy with colostomy D. Barium swallow E. Computed tomography
Answer: B — Sigmoidoscopy with flatus tube insertion
Why? In sigmoid volvulus without signs of gangrene or perforation, initial management is endoscopic detorsion using a sigmoidoscope and flatus tube. This is minimally invasive and can decompress the bowel. If unsuccessful or if gangrene is present, surgery is indicated.
High-Yield: Sigmoid volvulus management algorithm: if no peritonitis → endoscopic detorsion with flatus tube. If successful, elective sigmoid colectomy during the same admission (or later) to prevent recurrence. If peritonitis or failed detorsion → emergency laparotomy.
Q37
The most common cause of large bowel obstruction in the developing world is:
A. Adhesions B. Intestinal parasites C. Volvulus D. Hernia E. Neoplasia
Answer: C — Volvulus
Why? In many developing countries, sigmoid volvulus is the leading cause of large bowel obstruction, often related to a high-fibre diet and chronic constipation. In developed nations, colorectal cancer is more common.
High-Yield: Large bowel obstruction causes: colorectal cancer, volvulus (sigmoid, caecal), diverticular stricture, hernia, faecal impaction. Volvulus is more common in Africa, Asia, and South America.
Q38
A 22-year-old man is brought to the emergency room after a house fire. He has burns around his mouth and his voice is hoarse, but breathing is unlabored. What is the most appropriate next step in his management?
A. Immediate endotracheal intubation B. Examination of oral cavity and pharynx, with fiberoptic laryngoscope if available C. Place on supplemental oxygen D. Placement of two large-bore IV catheters with fluid resuscitation E. Do an immediate Chest X-Ray
Answer: B — Examination of oral cavity and pharynx, with fiberoptic laryngoscope if available
Why? Signs of inhalation injury (facial burns, hoarseness, singed nasal hairs, carbonaceous sputum) warrant urgent airway assessment. If breathing is unlabored, immediate intubation is not required, but direct visualisation of the airway (laryngoscopy) is the next step to assess for oedema and need for intubation.
High-Yield: Inhalation injury increases mortality. Early intubation is indicated for stridor, hoarseness, respiratory distress, or extensive facial/neck burns. Do not wait for symptoms to worsen.

Q40
An 8-year-old boy is brought to the emergency room after accidentally touching a hot iron with his forearm. On examination, the burned area has weeping blisters and is very tender to the touch. What is the burn depth?
A. First degree B. Second degree C. Third degree D. Fourth degree E. A and B
Answer: B — Second degree (partial thickness)
Why? Blistering and severe tenderness are hallmarks of superficial partial-thickness (second-degree) burns. First-degree burns are erythematous and painful but do not blister. Third-degree burns are painless and leathery.
High-Yield: Burn depth: 1st = erythema, painful, no blisters. 2nd superficial = blisters, moist, very painful. 2nd deep = less painful, blanches slowly. 3rd = dry, leathery, insensate. 4th = involves fascia/muscle/bone.
Q41

Why? Unilateral bloody nipple discharge is most commonly caused by intraductal papilloma (benign), but the second most common cause is intraductal carcinoma (DCIS). Paget's disease presents with eczematous nipple changes, not bloody discharge.
High-Yield: Bloody nipple discharge: most common cause is intraductal papilloma. Other causes: duct ectasia, carcinoma. Investigation: ductography, cytology, microdochectomy. Any bloody discharge requires evaluation to exclude malignancy.
Q80
Which of the following conditions is associated with increased risk of breast cancer?
A. Atypical ductal hyperplasia B. Fibromatosis C. Fibroadenoma D. Papillomatosis E. Severe hyperplasia
Answer: A — Atypical ductal hyperplasia
Why? Atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) is a known risk factor for breast cancer (4–5 times increased risk). Fibroadenoma, fibromatosis, and simple papillomatosis without atypia are not associated with significantly increased risk.
High-Yield: Breast cancer risk factors: ADH, atypical lobular hyperplasia (ALH), lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS), family history, BRCA mutations, early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, obesity, alcohol.
Q81
Which of the following is not an absolute contraindication to breast conserving surgery?
A. Previous breast radiation B. Multicentric breast cancer C. Multifocal breast cancer D. Pregnancy E. Severe cardiopulmonary disease
Answer: E — Severe cardiopulmonary disease
Why? Severe cardiopulmonary disease is a relative contraindication to any surgery but not an absolute contraindication to breast-conserving surgery. Previous breast radiation, multicentric/multifocal disease, and pregnancy (especially first trimester) are absolute contraindications.
High-Yield: Contraindications to breast-conserving surgery: multicentric disease, prior radiation to breast, pregnancy (first trimester), positive margins after re-excision, inflammatory breast cancer. Relative: large tumour size, collagen vascular disease.
Q82
A 28-year-old man presents to his GP with a 4 cm mobile anterior neck mass. A fine needle aspirate is performed which reveals cells with 'Orphan Annie eye' nuclei and psammoma bodies. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Papillary thyroid carcinoma B. Follicular thyroid carcinoma C. Medullary thyroid carcinoma D. Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma E. Parathyroid carcinoma
Answer: A — Papillary thyroid carcinoma
Why? "Orphan Annie eye" nuclei (optically clear nuclei) and psammoma bodies are classic cytological features of papillary thyroid carcinoma. It is the most common thyroid malignancy.
High-Yield: Papillary thyroid carcinoma: most common, spreads via lymphatics, excellent prognosis. Follicular: spreads haematogenously (bone, lung). Medullary: from parafollicular C cells, secretes calcitonin. Anaplastic: aggressive, poor prognosis.
Q83
A 46-year-old female presents to the preadmission clinic and is found to have a 3 cm anterolateral firm painless neck mass along with a minor decrease in calcium serum levels. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Anaplastic carcinoma B. Papillary thyroid carcinoma C. Medullary thyroid carcinoma D. Follicular thyroid carcinoma E. Parathyroid carcinoma
Answer: D — Follicular thyroid carcinoma
Why? A painless, firm thyroid nodule with mild hypocalcaemia suggests a follicular neoplasm. Follicular carcinoma can be associated with hypocalcaemia due to bone metastases (osteolytic). Papillary carcinoma is more common but usually euthyroid. Parathyroid carcinoma would cause hypercalcaemia, not hypocalcaemia.
High-Yield: Follicular thyroid carcinoma: often presents as solitary nodule, haematogenous spread to bone and lungs. Diagnosis requires capsular invasion on histology (FNA cannot distinguish follicular adenoma from carcinoma). Treatment: thyroidectomy.
Q84
Which is the most common type of thyroid carcinoma?
A. Follicular carcinoma B. Papillary carcinoma C. Medullary carcinoma D. Anaplastic carcinoma E. Hurthle cell carcinoma
Answer: B — Papillary carcinoma
Why? Papillary thyroid carcinoma is the most common thyroid malignancy, accounting for ~80% of cases. It has the best prognosis.
High-Yield: Thyroid cancer types: papillary (80%, good prognosis, lymphatic spread), follicular (10%, haematogenous), medullary (5%, MEN syndromes, calcitonin), anaplastic (1–2%, poor prognosis). Hurthle cell is a variant of follicular.
Q85
A 10-year-old boy is brought to the emergency department via ambulance after he was involved in a motor vehicle collision. The patient is unconscious on arrival. Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score is 8. CT scan does not show any specific lesion. The patient remains unconscious for the next 7 hours. Based on these findings, which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Subdural hematoma B. Diffuse axonal injury C. Epidural hematoma
Answer: B — Diffuse axonal injury
Why? Diffuse axonal injury (DAI) is characterised by prolonged unconsciousness after trauma with no focal lesion on CT. It results from shearing forces on axons. CT may be normal or show small petechial haemorrhages. Prognosis is variable.
High-Yield: DAI: mechanism of acceleration/deceleration, common in high-speed MVAs. Clinical: coma without focal signs, often with brainstem signs. MRI is more sensitive (diffusion tensor imaging). Treatment: supportive.
Q86
A patient who sustained a traumatic brain injury has impaired consciousness, Glasgow Coma Scale score of <15, focal neurologic findings, and suspected fracture. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial imaging step?
A. CT scan B. MRI C. Skull X-ray D. Cranial Ultrasound E. Magnetic resonance angiography
Answer: A — CT scan
Why? Non-contrast CT head is the initial imaging of choice for traumatic brain injury. It is rapid, widely available, and can detect acute haemorrhage, fractures, and mass effect. MRI is more sensitive for subtle injuries but takes longer and is less available in emergencies.
High-Yield: CT head is the gold standard initial imaging for TBI. Indications for CT: GCS <15, focal neurological deficit, suspected skull fracture, post-traumatic seizure, anticoagulation, age >65. Skull X-ray is no longer routinely recommended.
Q87
Class III shock results from ___ % of acute blood loss.
A. Greater than 40% B. 30–40% C. 15–20% D. Less than 15% E. 1000 mL to 1500 mL
Answer: B — 30–40%
Why? ATLS classification of haemorrhagic shock: Class I = <15% (up to 750 mL), Class II = 15–30% (750–1500 mL), Class III = 30–40% (1500–2000 mL), Class IV = >40% (>2000 mL). Class III is characterised by hypotension, tachycardia, decreased pulse pressure, and altered mental status.
High-Yield: ATLS shock classification: I (compensated), II (tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure), III (hypotension, tachypnoea, confusion), IV (profound shock, lethargy). Treatment: two large-bore IV lines, crystalloid bolus, blood products for Class III/IV.
Q88
The goal of fluid resuscitation is:
A. Only achieved with central venous access B. Restore adequate tissue perfusion C. To provide an initial infusion of 2 liters of crystalloids for all patients D. To only administer colloids E. To administer 20 mL/kg bolus of crystalloid fluid
Answer: B — Restore adequate tissue perfusion
Why? The primary goal of fluid resuscitation is to restore adequate tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery, not just to give a fixed volume. Endpoints include normalisation of vital signs, urine output, mental status, and lactate clearance. Central access is not always required; peripheral lines suffice initially.
High-Yield: Fluid resuscitation targets: MAP ≥65 mmHg, urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/hr, normalising lactate, improved mental status. Crystalloids are first-line. Colloids are not superior and may be harmful in some cases (e.g., albumin in TBI, starches in sepsis).
