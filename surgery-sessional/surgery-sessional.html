Q1
Which of the following statements is not correct?
A. The normal pulse can be 54 beats per minute B. Trachea deviation in tension pneumothorax is best seen on a chest x-ray during primary survey C. A wide pulse pressure may be seen in a patient with erectile dysfunction D. Hooking of the ribs in flail chest should be done in theatre under general anaesthesia E. A cardiac glycoside may be given in a patient with a thoracic trauma
Answer: B — Trachea deviation in tension pneumothorax is best seen on a chest x-ray during primary survey
Why? A tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis and a life-threatening emergency. You should never wait for a chest X-ray to confirm the diagnosis or to see tracheal deviation, as this delays life-saving needle decompression. The diagnosis is made clinically (hypotension, absent breath sounds, distended neck veins, tracheal deviation). A normal pulse can be 54 bpm in athletes (A is true). Wide pulse pressure can occur with erectile dysfunction drugs or conditions (C is true). Flail chest rib fixation is done in theatre (D is true). Cardiac glycosides (digoxin) can be used for rate control in AF post-trauma (E is true).
High-Yield: Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis. Immediate needle decompression is the treatment of choice. Chest X-ray is contraindicated in an unstable patient with suspected tension pneumothorax.

Q4
Regarding hernias:
A. An indirect inguinal hernia does not pass through the inguinal canal B. There is a potential space in the Hasselbach's triangle where a Lumbar hernia protrudes C. Indirect inguinal hernia follows pathway that testicles made during prebirth D. A direct hernia will protrude into the scrotum E. Large hernias strangulate easily
Answer: C — Indirect inguinal hernia follows pathway that testicles made during prebirth
Why? An indirect inguinal hernia passes through the deep inguinal ring, the inguinal canal, and out the superficial ring. It follows the path of the processus vaginalis, which is the pathway the testicles took during descent. A direct hernia protrudes through Hesselbach's triangle (not lumbar). Direct hernias rarely enter the scrotum. Small, narrow-necked hernias (like femoral) strangulate more easily than large ones.
High-Yield: Indirect inguinal hernias are congenital (patent processus vaginalis) and more common in younger patients. Direct hernias are acquired (weakness of the posterior wall) and more common in older men.

Q6

Answer: A — The median sacral artery is a single branch of the abdominal aorta
Why? The median sacral artery is a small, unpaired branch arising from the posterior aspect of the abdominal aorta just above its bifurcation. Q7
The following is the most common cause of generalized lymphadenopathy in Zambia:
A. Metastatic malignant disease B. Hand abscess C. Disseminated Tuberculosis D. Kaposi's disease E. Lymphoma
Answer: C — Disseminated Tuberculosis
Why? In Zambia, an endemic area for tuberculosis and HIV, disseminated (miliary) TB is a very common cause of generalized lymphadenopathy. HIV itself can also cause generalized lymphadenopathy, and TB is a common co-infection. Lymphoma and metastatic cancer are important differentials but less common overall.
High-Yield: Generalized lymphadenopathy in HIV-endemic regions should prompt investigation for TB and HIV. Fine needle aspiration (FNA) or excisional biopsy (preferred for lymphoma) is diagnostic.

Why? Q10
A 30-year-old man is brought to the emergency department following a high-speed car accident. He was the driver, and the windshield of the car was broken. On examination, he is alert, awake, oriented, and in no respiratory distress. He is unable to move any of his four extremities; however, his extremities are warm and pink. His vital signs on admission are HR 54 bpm and BP 70/40 mm Hg. What is the diagnosis?
A. Hemorrhagic shock B. Cardiogenic shock C. Neurogenic shock D. Septic shock E. Irreversible shock
Answer: C — Neurogenic shock
Why? The patient has a high cervical spinal cord injury (quadriplegia, warm/pink extremities) with hypotension and bradycardia. This is the classic presentation of neurogenic shock, which occurs due to loss of sympathetic tone (vasodilation and loss of cardiac sympathetic innervation). Hemorrhagic shock would present with tachycardia and cold, clammy extremities.
High-Yield: Neurogenic shock: hypotension + bradycardia + warm extremities (due to vasodilation). Treatment: IV fluids, vasopressors (e.g., norepinephrine or phenylephrine), and atropine for bradycardia. Rule out other causes of shock (e.g., hemorrhage) first.

Q12
A comatose patient who has sustained multiple closed injuries is admitted with severe hypotension. The hypotension is most probably due to:
A. Intracranial hematoma B. Cerebral concussion C. Internal hemorrhage D. Neurogenic shock E. Cardiogenic shock
Answer: C — Internal hemorrhage
Why? In a trauma patient with multiple closed injuries, the most common cause of severe hypotension is hemorrhage (internal or external). The patient is comatose, which could be due to head injury, but intracranial hematoma or concussion alone rarely cause severe hypotension (unless there is brainstem herniation, which is a late sign). Q14
The following is a feature of venous disorder:
A. Claudication B. Rest pain C. Ulceration D. Numbness E. Dry gangrene of the 5th toe
Answer: C — Ulceration
Why? Chronic venous insufficiency can lead to venous ulcers, typically located in the gaiter area (medial lower leg, above the medial malleolus). They are usually shallow, with sloping edges, and associated with edema, hyperpigmentation, and lipodermatosclerosis. Claudication, rest pain, and dry gangrene are features of arterial insufficiency, not venous disease.
High-Yield: Venous ulcers: shallow, irregular borders, moist, painful, located medially. Arterial ulcers: deep, punched-out, dry, painful, located on pressure points (toes, lateral malleolus, heel).
Q15
Changes in the body metabolism that occur in response to trauma are the following except:
A. Lipolysis B. Gluconeogenesis C. Protein breakdown D. Hypoglycemia E. Hypermetabolism
Answer: D — Hypoglycemia
Why? The metabolic response to trauma is characterized by a hypermetabolic, catabolic state driven by the release of stress hormones (cortisol, catecholamines, glucagon). This leads to lipolysis, gluconeogenesis, protein breakdown, and hypermetabolism. Blood glucose levels typically rise (hyperglycemia), not fall (hypoglycemia), due to increased gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance.
High-Yield: The "ebb and flow" phases of trauma: Ebb phase (early shock, hypometabolism) and Flow phase (catabolic, hypermetabolism). Hyperglycemia in trauma is a marker of severity and is associated with worse outcomes.
Q16

Answer: E — All of the above
Why? A groin swelling has a wide differential diagnosis. Q18
The following are colloids except:
A. Dextran B. Gelofusine C. Hetastarch D. Albumin E. Lactated Ringers
Answer: E — Lactated Ringers
Why? Lactated Ringers is a crystalloid solution, not a colloid. Crystalloids are solutions of small molecules (electrolytes) that can cross the capillary membrane. Colloids contain large molecules (e.g., albumin, dextran, gelatin, hetastarch) that stay in the intravascular space longer.
High-Yield: Colloids are used for volume expansion but are more expensive and have side effects (e.g., anaphylaxis with dextran/gelatin, coagulopathy with hetastarch). Crystalloids are the first-line resuscitation fluid.
Q19
Crystalloid solutions:
A. Are like plasma B. Have bigger molecules C. An example is Dextrose saline D. They stay in blood for a longer period E. Blood products are examples
Answer: C — An example is Dextrose saline
Why? Dextrose saline (e.g., 5% dextrose in 0.9% saline) is a crystalloid solution. Crystalloids have small molecules that can cross the capillary membrane, so they do not stay in the intravascular space for long (unlike colloids). They are not exactly like plasma (they lack proteins and other components). Blood products are not crystalloids.
High-Yield: Examples of crystalloids: Normal saline (0.9% NaCl), Ringer's lactate, Dextrose 5% in water (D5W), Dextrose saline. They distribute mainly into the extracellular fluid (ECF) compartment.

Q21
The following statement is false about Fat embolism:

Q22

Answer: C — Colostomy

Q24
The following is a spontaneous cause of enterocutaneous fistula:
A. Endoscopic operation B. Ulcerative colitis C. Iatrogenic surgical trauma to the bowel D. Intra-abdominal sepsis E. Disruption of anastomosis
Answer: B — Ulcerative colitis
Why? Enterocutaneous fistulas can be spontaneous (due to underlying disease) or acquired (postoperative). Q25
The complications of Parotidectomy include all the following except:
A. Injury to the facial nerve B. Earlobe numbness C. Reduced saliva production D. Frey syndrome E. Sialocoele
Answer: C — Reduced saliva production
Why? Parotidectomy (removal of the parotid gland) commonly causes injury to the facial nerve (which runs through the gland), numbness of the earlobe (greater auricular nerve injury), Frey syndrome (gustatory sweating due to aberrant nerve regeneration), and sialocele (salivary fluid collection). However, the other major salivary glands (submandibular, sublingual, and minor glands) compensate, so reduced saliva production (xerostomia) is not a typical complication.
High-Yield: The facial nerve branches are at risk during parotid surgery. A nerve monitor is often used. Frey syndrome is treated with botulinum toxin injections or surgical interposition of a barrier (e.g., fascia).

Q28
Granulation tissue is composed of the following:
A. Neutrophils B. Macrophages C. Newly formed blood vessels D. Monocytes E. Necrotic tissue
Answer: C — Newly formed blood vessels
Why? Granulation tissue is the new connective tissue and microscopic blood vessels that form on the surface of a wound during the healing process. It is composed of newly formed blood vessels (angiogenesis), fibroblasts, and inflammatory cells (macrophages). It does not contain necrotic tissue (which would impair healing).
High-Yield: Granulation tissue appears pink/red and granular. It fills the wound defect and provides a scaffold for epithelialization. Poor granulation tissue (pale, flat) may indicate infection, ischemia, or malnutrition.
Q29
A localised collection of pus is composed of the following except:
A. Degenerate leucocytes B. Dead and degenerate host tissue cells C. Oedema fluid D. Dead microorganisms E. Mononuclear phagocytes
Answer: E — Mononuclear phagocytes
Why? Pus (a localized collection of pus = abscess) is composed of degenerate (dead) neutrophils (not mononuclear phagocytes, which are macrophages/monocytes), dead tissue cells, edema fluid, and dead microorganisms. Mononuclear phagocytes are present in chronic inflammation (e.g., granulomas) rather than acute suppurative (pus-forming) infections.
High-Yield: Pus is a hallmark of acute bacterial infection. The predominant cell is the neutrophil. Abscesses require drainage because antibiotics cannot penetrate the pus-filled cavity effectively.

Answer: E — Negative transillumination is a feature of a cold abscess
Why? A cold abscess (e.g., tuberculous abscess) contains caseous material (pus) and does not transilluminate (it is opaque). Transillumination is positive in cystic, fluid-filled swellings like hydroceles. Autoimmune diseases are not directly caused by infections (though infections can trigger them). Pronation is a movement of the forearm (radioulnar joints), not the elbow joint. A large pleural effusion may require drainage, but an underwater seal drain is specifically for pneumothorax/hemothorax (chest drainage), not simple effusion. Prolactin is present in men.
High-Yield: Cold abscess: painless, fluctuant swelling without signs of acute inflammation. Common in TB. Diagnosis: aspiration (AFB, culture, PCR). Treatment: anti-TB drugs; may require aspiration or surgical drainage if large.
Q32
Regarding the breast:
A. The axillary tail of Spence is superficial to the deep fascia B. Glands of Montgomery give support to the nipple C. Lactiferous ducts drain the lobules D. Receives its blood supply from the lateral thoracic artery E. Cancers are common in the inner lower quadrant
Answer: C — Lactiferous ducts drain the lobules
Why? The functional unit of the breast is the lobule, which drains via lactiferous ducts to the nipple. The axillary tail of Spence extends through the deep fascia into the axilla. Glands of Montgomery are sebaceous glands in the areola (they lubricate, not support). The breast receives blood from the internal thoracic, lateral thoracic, and intercostal arteries. Cancers are most common in the upper outer quadrant.
High-Yield: The upper outer quadrant is the most common site for breast cancer (about 50% of cases) because it contains the most glandular tissue (axillary tail of Spence). Lymphatic drainage is primarily to the axillary nodes.
Q33
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF):
A. Is the main source of brain nutrition B. Is mainly produced by active secretion from the spinal cord C. Contains virtually no glucose D. pH changes rapidly in response to changes in plasma pH E. Pressure increases with jugular venous obstruction
Answer: E — Pressure increases with jugular venous obstruction
Why? CSF is produced mainly by the choroid plexus in the ventricles. It contains glucose (about 60% of plasma levels) and has a stable pH. Because CSF drains into the venous system (via arachnoid villi), obstruction of the jugular veins (e.g., by a tumor or thrombosis) impairs CSF absorption, leading to increased intracranial pressure.
High-Yield: Normal CSF: clear, colorless, glucose 50-80 mg/dL, protein 15-45 mg/dL, WBC 0-5 cells/µL. In bacterial meningitis: elevated WBC (neutrophils), high protein, low glucose. In viral meningitis: elevated WBC (lymphocytes), normal glucose.
Q34
Following insertion of the chest drain, emergency thoracotomy is indicated for a blood loss of:
A. Greater than 800 mls of blood at insertion B. Greater than 200 mls/hr for 4 consecutive hours C. Greater than 100 mls/hr for greater than 6 hours D. About 800 mls in 24 hrs and the pulse of 115 bpm in an adult E. Greater than 1500 mls at insertion or >200 mls/hr for 2-4 hours
Answer: E — Greater than 1500 mls at insertion or >200 mls/hr for 2-4 hours (Note: The options in the original paper are garbled. The standard criteria for thoracotomy in massive hemothorax are: >1500 mL immediately, or >200 mL/hr for 2-4 hours. Option B is the closest available option in the list, though E is the classic textbook answer. I will explain the standard criteria and note the closest option.)
Why? Emergency thoracotomy is indicated for massive hemothorax if: 1) >1500 mL of blood is evacuated immediately upon chest tube insertion, or 2) >200 mL/hour of blood drains for 2-4 consecutive hours, or if the patient remains hemodynamically unstable. The other options do not meet the standard criteria.
High-Yield: Massive hemothorax is a life-threatening emergency. Initial management: chest tube insertion (large bore, e.g., 32-36 Fr). If the criteria for thoracotomy are met, urgent surgical exploration is required to control bleeding (e.g., from intercostal vessels, lung parenchyma, or great vessels).
Q35
Cold abscesses are seen in all the following conditions except:
A. Tuberculosis B. Actinomycosis C. Madura foot D. Leprosy E. Pyaemic abscess
Answer: E — Pyaemic abscess
Why? A cold abscess is a collection of pus without the classic signs of acute inflammation (redness, heat, severe pain). It is typical of chronic infections like tuberculosis (caseous necrosis). Actinomycosis, madura foot, and leprosy can also produce chronic, cold-type abscesses or granulomas. A pyaemic abscess (from pyaemia, a type of sepsis with multiple abscesses) is an acute, suppurative infection with classic inflammatory signs (hot, red, tender), not a cold abscess.
High-Yield: Cold abscess management: aspiration (for diagnosis and sometimes treatment) and anti-tuberculous therapy. Incision and drainage can lead to a chronic sinus tract and should be avoided unless necessary.
Q36
The following conditions are systemic manifestations of acute pancreatitis except:
A. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) B. Acute renal failure C. Myocardial infarction D. Hemorrhage E. Hypotensive shock
Answer: C — Myocardial infarction
Why? Severe acute pancreatitis can lead to a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), causing distant organ failure. Systemic complications include ARDS, acute renal failure, hypotensive shock (due to third-spacing and SIRS), and hemorrhage (e.g., from erosion of splenic or gastroduodenal vessels). Myocardial infarction is not a direct systemic manifestation of pancreatitis (though cardiac ischemia can occur secondary to severe shock).
High-Yield: Local complications of pancreatitis: pancreatic necrosis, pseudocyst, abscess, hemorrhage (Grey-Turner's or Cullen's sign). Systemic complications: shock, ARDS, renal failure, hypocalcemia, hyperglycemia.
Q37
The following option is false about approximate daily volumes produced of various gastrointestinal fluids:
A. Bile is 2.5 L B. Gastric juice is 2.5 L C. Pancreatic juice is 1.5 L D. Mature ileostomy 500 mls E. Saliva is 500 mls
Answer: E — Saliva is 500 mls
Why? Normal daily salivary production is approximately 1.0 to 1.5 Liters, not 500 mL. Gastric juice is about 2-3 L, pancreatic juice 1.5-2 L, and bile 0.5-1 L (though some texts say up to 2.5 L). A mature ileostomy typically outputs 500-1000 mL/day. Therefore, the statement that saliva is 500 mL is false.
High-Yield: Massive fluid and electrolyte losses can occur from high-output stomas (e.g., ileostomy >1.5 L/day) or fistulas, leading to dehydration, hypokalemia, and hyponatremia. Management requires IV fluids, oral rehydration solutions, and anti-motility agents.
Q38
Gastric outlet obstruction:
A. Can be caused by vomiting B. Presents with hypochloremic respiratory alkalosis C. Retained gastric material can be detected by a succession splash D. Water soluble contrast should be done before gastroscopy E. Initial management does not require fluid resuscitation
Answer: C — Retained gastric material can be detected by a succession splash
Why? Gastric outlet obstruction (e.g., from peptic ulcer disease or gastric cancer) leads to the accumulation of gastric contents. On physical examination, a succession splash can be elicited by rocking the patient's abdomen, indicating retained fluid and food in the stomach. Vomiting is a symptom, not a cause. The metabolic disturbance is hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis (not respiratory). Gastroscopy is the investigation of choice and should be done after initial resuscitation (NG tube decompression, IV fluids).
High-Yield: Initial management of gastric outlet obstruction: nothing by mouth, NG tube decompression, aggressive IV fluid resuscitation (normal saline with potassium), and correction of electrolyte abnormalities. Then, endoscopy for diagnosis (biopsy if malignancy suspected) and balloon dilation or surgery if benign stricture.
Q39
The following statement is not true about Acute appendicitis:
A. Diagnosed by pain starting in the right iliac fossa associated with low-grade fever and leucocytosis B. Is commoner in young people C. Leads to one of the commonest abdominal operations D. Is linked to severe complications in a minority irrespective of age E. Is associated with inflammatory bowel disease
Answer: A — Diagnosed by pain starting in the right iliac fossa associated with low-grade fever and leucocytosis
Why? The classic presentation of acute appendicitis is periumbilical pain that migrates to the right lower quadrant (RLQ), not pain that starts in the RLQ. The migration of pain is a key diagnostic feature. Appendicitis is common in young people, is the most common abdominal surgical emergency, and can have severe complications (perforation, abscess). It is not directly caused by inflammatory bowel disease (though Crohn's can mimic it).
High-Yield: Alvarado score (MANTRELS): Migration of pain, Anorexia, Nausea/vomiting, Tenderness in RLQ, Rebound tenderness, Elevated temperature, Leukocytosis, Shift to left. A score of 7-10 indicates high probability and often warrants appendectomy.
Q40
Which one of the descriptions of fractures below is least likely to be correct?
A. An acute fracture has fragments with sharp, well-defined edges B. A fracture may be an incomplete disruption of the normal bony architecture C. It is a Salter-Harris type IV injury if the growth plate is crushed D. It is difficult to see a cartilage fracture on an X-ray (radiograph) E. There is a volar angulation in Smith's fracture
Answer: C — It is a Salter-Harris type IV injury if the growth plate is crushed
Why? A crush injury to the growth plate is a Salter-Harris Type V injury, not Type IV. Type IV involves a fracture through the metaphysis, physis, and epiphysis. Type V has a poor prognosis due to the risk of growth arrest. Acute fractures do have sharp edges. Incomplete fractures (e.g., greenstick) occur in children. Cartilage is radiolucent, so fractures are hard to see on X-ray. Smith's fracture has volar angulation.
High-Yield: Salter-Harris classification: I = through physis only; II = physis + metaphysis (most common); III = physis + epiphysis; IV = metaphysis + physis + epiphysis; V = crush injury.
Q41
Flexion of the distal interphalangeal joint while fixing the PIP joint tests:
A. Palmaris longus B. Flexor digitorum profundus C. Flexor digitorum superficialis D. Extensor carpi radialis E. Brachioradialis
Answer: B — Flexor digitorum profundus
Why? The flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) is the only muscle that flexes the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint. To test it in isolation, the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint is held in extension (fixed). The flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) flexes the PIP joint; it is tested by holding the other fingers in extension and asking the patient to flex the PIP joint of the affected finger.
High-Yield: FDP is innervated by the anterior interosseous nerve (AIN, branch of median nerve) and ulnar nerve. FDS is innervated by the median nerve. Test FDS by asking the patient to flex the PIP joint while the other fingers are held extended (to eliminate FDP action).
Q42
Trendelenburg test is positive due to injury of which nerve(s)?
A. Superior gluteal nerve B. Inferior gluteal nerve C. Pudendal nerve D. Obturator nerve E. Sural nerve
Answer: A — Superior gluteal nerve
Why? The Trendelenburg sign occurs when there is weakness of the hip abductors (gluteus medius and minimus), which are innervated by the superior gluteal nerve (L4, L5, S1). When the patient stands on one leg, the pelvis drops on the contralateral side due to the inability to stabilize the pelvis.
High-Yield: Superior gluteal nerve injury can occur from pelvic fractures, intramuscular injections (especially in the buttock), or during hip surgery. The patient may also have a positive Trendelenburg gait (lurching toward the affected side).
Q43
In carpal tunnel syndrome, which of the following is not true?
A. It can be treated by corticosteroids B. The nerve compressed is radial C. The nerve compressed is median D. Hypothyroidism is the cause E. There is thenar muscle wasting if chronic and severe
Answer: B — The nerve compressed is radial
Why? Carpal tunnel syndrome is caused by compression of the median nerve as it passes through the carpal tunnel in the wrist. The radial nerve is not involved. Corticosteroid injections can be used for treatment. Hypothyroidism is a known risk factor (myxedema). Chronic severe compression leads to thenar muscle wasting (supplied by the recurrent branch of the median nerve).
High-Yield: CTS symptoms: numbness/tingling in the thumb, index, middle, and radial half of ring finger (median nerve distribution), worse at night. Tinel's and Phalen's tests are positive. Treatment: splinting, steroid injection, surgical release (carpal tunnel release).
Q44
Metastatic bone tumours commonly arise from all the following except:
A. Breast cancer B. Thyroid cancer C. Colon cancer D. Malignant melanoma E. Prostate
Answer: C — Colon cancer
Why? The most common cancers that metastasize to bone are (in order of frequency): Prostate, Breast, Kidney, Lung, and Thyroid (remember: "PB KTL" or "Lead kettle"). Colon cancer rarely metastasizes to bone; it typically spreads to the liver and lungs. Malignant melanoma can also metastasize to bone, but less commonly than the others.
High-Yield: Bone metastases are typically osteolytic (breast, lung, kidney, thyroid) or osteoblastic (prostate). They cause bone pain, pathological fractures, hypercalcemia, and spinal cord compression. Treatment: bisphosphonates, radiotherapy, surgery for stabilization.
Q45
Immobilisation is required in fracture involving:
A. Scapula B. Wings of the ilium C. Ribs D. Skull E. Salter Harris fracture
Answer: E — Salter Harris fracture
Why? Salter-Harris fractures involve the growth plate (physis) and require careful immobilization to prevent further injury and allow proper healing, minimizing the risk of growth disturbance. Scapula, iliac wing, and skull fractures often do not require rigid immobilization (they are managed conservatively or with protective measures). Rib fractures are typically managed with analgesia and breathing exercises, not immobilization (to avoid pneumonia).
High-Yield: Salter-Harris fractures are classified I-V. Types I and II are usually managed conservatively with cast immobilization. Types III and IV often require anatomical reduction and internal fixation to restore joint congruity and prevent growth arrest.
Q46
Compound fracture of tibia with skin loss is best treated by:
A. Intramedullary nailing B. External fixation C. Complete Plaster cast D. Internal fixation E. Splinting with skeletal traction
Answer: B — External fixation
Why? A compound (open) fracture with significant skin loss (Gustilo Type III) has a high risk of infection. External fixation is preferred because it provides stable fixation while allowing access to the wound for dressing changes, debridement, and soft tissue coverage (e.g., flaps). Internal fixation (plates/screws) or intramedullary nailing carries a higher risk of deep infection in this setting.
High-Yield: Open fracture management: 1) IV antibiotics (within 1 hour), 2) Tetanus prophylaxis, 3) Urgent surgical debridement and irrigation, 4) Stabilization (external fixator for severe soft tissue injury, IM nail for less severe), 5) Soft tissue coverage (early).
Q47
A fifty-year-old man presents to casualty following a fall from a height. He is unconscious and has an open skull fracture. What is your first important step?
A. Secure the airways B. Control bleeding C. Send for CT scan D. Apply cervical collar E. Insert a urethral catheter
Answer: A — Secure the airway
Why? In any trauma patient, especially an unconscious one, the first priority is the airway (A in ABCDE). An unconscious patient cannot protect their airway and is at risk of hypoxia and aspiration. The airway must be secured (e.g., intubation) before addressing other issues like bleeding or getting a CT scan.
High-Yield: Primary survey: Airway with C-spine protection, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure. In an unconscious trauma patient, assume a cervical spine injury and protect the C-spine while securing the airway.
Q48
The most common fracture location to cause radial nerve injury is the:
A. Humeral neck B. Middle 1/3 of the Humeral shaft C. Supracondylar fractures D. Smith's fracture E. Radial head fractures
Answer: B — Middle 1/3 of the Humeral shaft
Why? The radial nerve winds around the posterior aspect of the humerus in the spiral groove, which is located in the middle third of the humeral shaft. Therefore, fractures in this region (e.g., Holstein-Lewis fracture) commonly injure the radial nerve, causing wrist drop.
High-Yield: Radial nerve injury: loss of wrist extension (wrist drop), loss of finger extension, and sensory loss over the dorsum of the hand (first web space). Humeral neck fractures more commonly injure the axillary nerve (deltoid weakness, numbness over deltoid).
Q49
The most desirable position to immobilize the hand is:
A. Wrist is flexed, MCP joints are extended and IP joints are flexed B. Wrist is flexed, MCP joints are flexed and IP joints are extended C. Wrist is slightly extended, MCP joints are flexed and the thumb slightly abducted D. Wrist is extended, MCP joints are flexed and IP joints are flexed E. Wrist is extended, MCP joints are flexed and the thumb flexed
Answer: C — Wrist is slightly extended, MCP joints are flexed and the thumb slightly abducted
Why? The "position of safe immobilization" (intrinsic plus position) is designed to prevent joint contractures and maintain the length of the collateral ligaments. The ideal position is: wrist slightly extended (20-30°), MCP joints flexed to 70-90°, and IP joints extended. The thumb should be abducted. This position prevents stiffness and preserves hand function.
High-Yield: Prolonged immobilization in a flat or extended hand position can lead to "claw hand" deformity due to MCP joint extension and IP joint flexion (lumbrical plus deformity). Always immobilize in the intrinsic plus position.
Q50
The following statement is not correct regarding multiple long bone fractures in a young man:
A. 1500 mls of blood may be lost from the femur B. 2000 mls of blood may be lost from the humerus C. Patient may complicate to Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome D. Open Reduction and Internal fixation is indicated E. Fat embolism is a possible complication
Answer: B — 2000 mls of blood may be lost from the humerus
Why? A closed humeral shaft fracture can lose about 500-750 mL of blood (up to 1000 mL). A femur fracture can lose 1000-2000 mL (up to 3 L in the thigh). Therefore, stating that 2000 mL can be lost from the humerus is incorrect. Multiple long bone fractures can lead to ARDS, fat embolism, and usually require early stabilization (ORIF or external fixation).
High-Yield: Estimated blood loss: pelvis 1-3 L, femur 1-2 L, humerus 500-750 mL, tibia 500-1000 mL, ribs 100-200 mL each. Always assess for hemorrhagic shock in major fractures.
Q51
Which of the following is incorrect about Osteoarthritis?
A. Can occur as primary idiopathic condition B. Overstressing of the joint in obesity is a predisposing factor C. Affects predominantly the non-weight bearing joints D. Articular cartilage thinning results into reduction of joint space E. Osteotomy is one of the treatment options
Answer: C — Affects predominantly the non-weight bearing joints
Why? Osteoarthritis (OA) predominantly affects weight-bearing joints such as the knees, hips, and spine, as well as the hands (DIP and PIP joints). It does not predominantly affect non-weight-bearing joints. Primary OA is idiopathic. Obesity is a major risk factor. Cartilage thinning leads to joint space narrowing on X-ray. Osteotomy (e.g., high tibial osteotomy) is a surgical option for young patients with unicompartmental knee OA.
High-Yield: OA is characterized by "wear and tear" changes: joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, osteophyte formation, and subchondral cysts. Symptoms: pain worse with activity, relieved by rest, morning stiffness <30 minutes.
Q52
All the following are differential diagnoses of a monoarthritis except:
A. Rheumatoid arthritis B. Osteoarthritis C. Gout D. Hemarthrosis E. Septic arthritis
Answer: A — Rheumatoid arthritis
Why? Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic inflammatory disease that typically presents as a polyarthritis (symmetrical involvement of multiple small joints, especially hands and wrists). It does not typically present as a monoarthritis (single joint). The other options can present as monoarthritis: osteoarthritis (e.g., knee), gout (e.g., big toe), hemarthrosis (trauma, bleeding disorders), and septic arthritis (acute single joint infection).
High-Yield: Differential diagnosis of acute monoarthritis: septic arthritis, gout/pseudogout, trauma/hemarthrosis, reactive arthritis, and flare of osteoarthritis. Always rule out septic arthritis first (joint aspiration and culture).
Q53
Trendelenburg test is positive due to injury of which nerve?
A. Superior gluteal nerve B. Inferior gluteal nerve C. Pudendal nerve D. Obturator nerve E. Sural nerve
Answer: A — Superior gluteal nerve
Why? This is a duplicate of Q42. The Trendelenburg sign is caused by weakness of the hip abductors (gluteus medius and minimus), which are innervated by the superior gluteal nerve (L4, L5, S1).
High-Yield: The inferior gluteal nerve innervates the gluteus maximus (hip extension). Injury causes difficulty rising from a seated position and climbing stairs. The obturator nerve innervates the adductors. The pudendal nerve innervates the perineum and external sphincters.
Q54
If a person falls from a height and lands on both feet, the pattern of fracture will most likely be:
A. Supracondylar fracture of the humerus B. Calcaneus fracture unilaterally C. Fracture of the femur D. Lumbar spine fracture E. Pilon fracture of the ankle
Answer: E — Pilon fracture of the ankle (or Calcaneus fracture bilaterally)
Why? A fall from a height landing on both feet transmits axial force upwards. This classically causes bilateral calcaneal fractures, and can also cause pilon fractures (tibial plafond fractures), tibial plateau fractures, and lumbar spine compression fractures. Among the options, a Pilon fracture (or calcaneus, though the option says unilaterally) is the most likely. Landing on both feet typically causes bilateral calcaneal fractures, not unilateral. A Pilon fracture is a fracture of the distal tibia involving the weight-bearing articular surface (tibial plafond), often caused by axial loading.
High-Yield: In a patient who falls from a height and lands on their feet, always examine both heels (for calcaneal fractures) and the lumbar spine (for compression fractures). "Don Juan" fracture: bilateral calcaneal fractures. Pilon fractures are often comminuted and require surgical fixation.
Q55
Which of the following statement regarding femoral neck fractures is TRUE?
A. They are more common in men B. Intracapsular fractures are more common than extracapsular fractures C. Displaced fractures should be treated with skin traction in the ED D. Displaced fractures in elderly patients are treated with arthroplasty E. They are commonly caused by high-energy trauma in the elderly
Answer: D — Displaced fractures in elderly patients are treated with arthroplasty
Why? Femoral neck fractures are more common in women (postmenopausal osteoporosis). Intracapsular fractures have a high risk of avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head. In elderly patients with displaced fractures, arthroplasty (hemiarthroplasty or total hip replacement) is the treatment of choice to allow early mobilization and avoid the high risk of AVN and non-union associated with internal fixation. Skin traction is not a definitive treatment. These fractures are usually caused by low-energy falls in the elderly.
High-Yield: Femoral neck fractures: intracapsular, high risk of AVN. Young patients: urgent internal fixation (cannulated screws). Elderly patients: arthroplasty. Intertrochanteric fractures: extracapsular, treated with sliding hip screw or cephalomedullary nail.
