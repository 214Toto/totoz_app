Section A: Multiple Choice Questions
Q1
A 45-year-old man presents with severe abdominal pain that began 8 hours ago. He describes it as sudden and sharp, localized to the epigastrium, and radiating to the back. On examination, he is diaphoretic and has abdominal tenderness with guarding. What is the possible differential diagnosis?
A. Acute Enteritis B. Acute Pancreatitis C. Acute Gastritis D. Acute cholecystitis
Answer: B — Acute Pancreatitis
Why? Epigastric pain radiating to the back is the hallmark of acute pancreatitis. Diaphoresis (sweating), tenderness, and guarding are consistent with an acute abdomen. Acute enteritis usually presents with diarrhea and periumbilical pain. Acute gastritis causes epigastric burning but rarely radiates to the back. Acute cholecystitis typically presents with right upper quadrant pain (Murphy's sign), often after fatty meals.
High-Yield: Acute pancreatitis: elevated amylase/lipase (>3x normal), caused by gallstones or alcohol. Management: aggressive IV fluids, analgesia, nil by mouth, and early enteral feeding when tolerated.
Q2
A 65-year-old woman presents with right upper quadrant pain, fever, and jaundice. Ultrasound shows gallstones and bile duct dilation. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Acute cholecystitis B. Choledocholithiasis C. Cholangitis (Charcot's Triad) D. Gallstone ileus
Answer: C — Cholangitis (Charcot's Triad)
Why? The combination of right upper quadrant (RUQ) pain, fever, and jaundice is known as Charcot's Triad, which is the classic presentation of acute ascending cholangitis (an infection of the biliary tree caused by obstruction, usually from a gallstone). Ultrasound confirms gallstones and bile duct dilation (obstruction).
High-Yield: Charcot's Triad = RUQ pain + Fever + Jaundice. Reynolds' Pentad = Charcot's Triad + Hypotension + Altered mental status (indicating suppurative cholangitis, a surgical emergency). Treatment: IV antibiotics + ERCP for drainage.
Q3
A 22-year-old male is brought to the emergency department after a motor vehicle collision. He is hypotensive with a systolic blood pressure of 80 mmHg and a pulse of 130/min. Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) reveals free intraperitoneal fluid. What is the most appropriate next step in management?
A. Observation B. Diagnostic peritoneal lavage C. Exploratory laparotomy
Answer: C — Exploratory laparotomy
Why? A positive FAST scan (free fluid) in a hemodynamically unstable patient (hypotension + tachycardia) is an indication for immediate exploratory laparotomy. The patient is likely bleeding internally and needs urgent surgical control of hemorrhage. Diagnostic peritoneal lavage (DPL) is largely obsolete in this setting since FAST is faster and non-invasive.
High-Yield: FAST is the gold standard for trauma triage. If unstable + positive FAST → OR (Laparotomy). If stable + positive FAST → CT scan for further evaluation. If unstable + negative FAST → look for other causes of shock (e.g., pelvic fracture, thoracic injury).
Q4
A 60-year-old man presents with a painless, progressive swelling in the inguinal region. Examination reveals a non-reducible mass that does not transilluminate. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Inguinal hernia B. Femoral hernia C. Lipoma D. Lymphadenopathy
Answer: A — Inguinal hernia
Why? A painless, progressive, non-reducible mass in the inguinal region that does not transilluminate (rules out hydrocele) is most likely an inguinal hernia. Femoral hernias are typically lower (below the inguinal ligament) and more common in women. Lipomas are usually soft and non-tender. Lymphadenopathy would present with multiple nodes, often tender if infective.
High-Yield: Indirect inguinal hernia: lateral to inferior epigastric vessels, through deep ring. Direct: medial to inferior epigastric vessels, through Hesselbach's triangle. Femoral hernia: below and lateral to pubic tubercle, higher risk of strangulation.
Q5
A 40-year-old man presents with severe chest pain that started 2 hours ago after vomiting. The pain is worsened by swallowing and radiates to his back. Chest X-ray shows mediastinal air. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Acute myocardial infarction B. Boerhaave syndrome (spontaneous rupture of oesophagus) C. Aortic dissection D. Pneumothorax
Answer: B — Boerhaave syndrome
Why? Boerhaave syndrome is a spontaneous full-thickness rupture of the esophagus, usually occurring after severe retching or vomiting. It presents with severe chest pain, often radiating to the back, worsened by swallowing. Mediastinal air (pneumomediastinum) on CXR is a classic sign. This is a surgical emergency requiring urgent repair.
High-Yield: Mackler's triad for Boerhaave: vomiting, chest pain, and subcutaneous emphysema. Contrast swallow (water-soluble) confirms the leak. Treatment: broad-spectrum antibiotics, IV fluids, and urgent surgical repair or stent placement.
Q6
A 34-year-old woman presents with a painful, erythematous lump in her breast. She is 6 weeks postpartum and breastfeeding. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Breast abscess B. Fibroadenoma C. Mastitis D. Galactocele
Answer: C — Mastitis
Why? A painful, erythematous, and swollen breast in a breastfeeding woman is classic for lactational mastitis, usually caused by Staphylococcus aureus entering through a cracked nipple. A breast abscess would present as a fluctuant mass. Fibroadenomas are painless and mobile. Galactoceles are milk-filled cysts, usually painless.
High-Yield: Mastitis treatment: continue breastfeeding (do not stop), warm compresses, and oral antibiotics (flucloxacillin or dicloxacillin). If an abscess forms (fluctuance, persistent fever), it requires ultrasound-guided aspiration or surgical incision and drainage.
Q7
A 30-year-old man presents with fever, right lower quadrant abdominal pain, and anorexia. Ultrasound reveals a dilated appendix with surrounding fluid collection. What is the next step in management?
A. Observation B. Intravenous antibiotics C. Appendectomy
Answer: C — Appendectomy
Why? The clinical picture and ultrasound findings are diagnostic of acute appendicitis with a possible peri-appendiceal fluid collection (early abscess or phlegmon). The definitive treatment is appendectomy (laparoscopic or open). Antibiotics alone are not standard of care for acute appendicitis in a fit patient, as there is a high risk of recurrence and perforation.
High-Yield: If a large abscess (>3 cm) is present, some surgeons may opt for percutaneous drainage followed by interval appendectomy (after 6–8 weeks). However, in the acute setting without a mature abscess, appendectomy is the gold standard.
Q8
A 65-year-old man with a history of atrial fibrillation presents with severe abdominal pain out of proportion to physical findings. Laboratory tests show elevated lactate. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Acute appendicitis B. Acute mesenteric ischemia C. Small bowel obstruction D. Acute pancreatitis
Answer: B — Acute mesenteric ischemia
Why? The classic presentation of acute mesenteric ischemia is pain out of proportion to physical examination (the abdomen may feel soft and non-tender despite severe pain). The history of atrial fibrillation is a major risk factor because it predisposes to embolic occlusion of the superior mesenteric artery. Elevated lactate indicates bowel ischemia and necrosis.
High-Yield: Mesenteric ischemia is a surgical emergency. CT angiography is the gold standard for diagnosis. Treatment: fluid resuscitation, IV antibiotics, and urgent surgical embolectomy or bowel resection. Mortality is very high (>60%).
Q9
A 50-year-old woman presents with progressive dysphagia to solids and liquids. Barium swallow shows a "bird-beak" appearance. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Achalasia B. Esophageal stricture C. Esophageal cancer D. Diffuse esophageal spasm
Answer: A — Achalasia
Why? A "bird-beak" appearance on barium swallow is the classic radiological sign of achalasia, caused by failure of the lower esophageal sphincter to relax and absent peristalsis in the esophageal body. Dysphagia to both solids and liquids is typical. Esophageal strictures and cancers usually cause progressive dysphagia initially to solids, then liquids.
High-Yield: Achalasia manometry: absent peristalsis + incomplete LES relaxation. Treatment: pneumatic dilation, laparoscopic Heller myotomy, or POEM. Botulinum toxin is reserved for patients unfit for surgery.
Q10
A 70-year-old man presents with a pulsatile abdominal mass and hypotension after collapsing at home. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Acute myocardial infarction B. Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm C. Acute pancreatitis D. Bowel perforation
Answer: B — Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)
Why? A pulsatile abdominal mass in an elderly male is highly suspicious for an abdominal aortic aneurysm. Sudden collapse with hypotension indicates rupture, which is a catastrophic surgical emergency with a high mortality rate. Immediate fluid resuscitation and transfer to theatre are required.
High-Yield: AAA screening: ultrasound for men >65 years. Ruptured AAA: classic triad of hypotension, pulsatile mass, and abdominal/back pain. Do NOT delay for CT if the patient is unstable; go straight to theatre.
Q11
A 25-year-old woman presents with chronic anal pain and discharge. Examination reveals a sinus tract near the anal verge. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Anal fissure B. Perianal abscess C. Fistula-in-ano D. Hemorrhoids
Answer: C — Fistula-in-ano
Why? A sinus tract near the anal verge that discharges pus or fluid is the hallmark of a fistula-in-ano, usually a complication of a previous perianal abscess. Anal fissures cause severe pain on defecation (tearing). Hemorrhoids present with painless bleeding or prolapse. A perianal abscess is an acute, painful, fluctuant swelling, not a chronic tract.
High-Yield: Goodsall's rule predicts fistula tract direction: anterior fistulas tract radially; posterior fistulas curve to the posterior midline. Treatment: fistulotomy or seton placement depending on the sphincter involvement.
Q12
A 45-year-old man presents with progressive difficulty swallowing, weight loss, and chest discomfort. Endoscopy reveals a mass in the lower third of the esophagus. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Barrett's esophagus B. Esophageal adenocarcinoma C. Achalasia D. Esophageal stricture
Answer: B — Esophageal adenocarcinoma
Why? The lower third of the esophagus is the most common site for esophageal adenocarcinoma, which typically arises from Barrett's esophagus (a complication of chronic GERD). Progressive dysphagia, weight loss, and a visible mass on endoscopy are classic.
High-Yield: Squamous cell carcinoma typically affects the upper/middle third of the esophagus (associated with smoking and alcohol). Adenocarcinoma affects the lower third (associated with GERD and Barrett's). Both present with progressive dysphagia and weight loss.
Q13
A 55-year-old woman presents with a rapidly growing breast mass and skin changes resembling an orange peel. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Fibroadenoma B. Inflammatory breast cancer C. Breast abscess D. Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)
Answer: B — Inflammatory breast cancer
Why? "Peau d'orange" (orange peel) skin changes, rapid growth, and erythema are classic signs of inflammatory breast cancer, an aggressive form of breast cancer where tumor cells block dermal lymphatic vessels. It is often mistaken for mastitis but does not respond to antibiotics.
High-Yield: Inflammatory breast cancer is a clinical diagnosis. A punch biopsy of the skin is often required to confirm dermal lymphatic invasion. Treatment: neoadjuvant chemotherapy, followed by mastectomy and radiation. It is a contraindication to breast-conserving surgery.
Q14
A 70-year-old man presents with colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, and obstipation for the past 2 days. Abdominal X-ray shows air-fluid levels and dilated loops of small bowel. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Paralytic ileus B. Small bowel obstruction C. Large bowel obstruction D. Volvulus
Answer: B — Small bowel obstruction
Why? Colicky pain, vomiting, obstipation (inability to pass flatus or stool), and dilated loops of small bowel with air-fluid levels on X-ray are the classic features of small bowel obstruction (SBO). Paralytic ileus would present with a silent abdomen (absent bowel sounds) and no colicky pain. Large bowel obstruction typically shows dilated colon. Volvulus has a specific "coffee bean" sign.
High-Yield: Most common cause of SBO: adhesions (post-surgical). Other causes: hernias, tumors, Crohn's disease. Management: IV fluids, NG decompression, and surgery if signs of strangulation (fever, tachycardia, peritoneal signs) or failure to resolve.
Q15
A 40-year-old woman presents with episodic right upper quadrant pain, nausea, and vomiting after fatty meals. Ultrasound shows gallstones without bile duct dilation. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Acute cholecystitis B. Biliary colic C. Cholangitis D. Gallstone ileus
Answer: B — Biliary colic
Why? Episodic RUQ pain after fatty meals, without fever or signs of inflammation, is typical of biliary colic. The gallbladder contracts after a fatty meal, and a stone temporarily obstructs the cystic duct, causing pain. There is no bile duct dilation (which would suggest choledocholithiasis) and no fever (which would suggest cholecystitis or cholangitis).
High-Yield: Biliary colic is a misnomer; it is a constant pain, not colicky. Treatment: elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy to prevent recurrence and complications (cholecystitis, pancreatitis, cholangitis).
Q16
A 60-year-old man with a history of alcohol abuse presents with a painless, palpable mass in the epigastrium. CT scan shows a fluid-filled cystic lesion in the pancreas. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Pancreatic pseudocyst B. Pancreatic abscess C. Cystic pancreatic tumor D. Chronic pancreatitis
Answer: A — Pancreatic pseudocyst
Why? A history of alcohol abuse (leading to pancreatitis) followed by a painless, palpable epigastric mass and a fluid-filled cystic lesion on CT is classic for a pancreatic pseudocyst. It is a collection of pancreatic fluid enclosed by a wall of fibrous tissue (not true epithelium).
High-Yield: Pancreatic pseudocysts usually form >4 weeks after acute pancreatitis. They can be observed if asymptomatic and <6 cm. If symptomatic or enlarging, they can be drained via endoscopy (cystogastrostomy) or surgery. Complications: infection, rupture, hemorrhage.
Q17
Which of the following muscles is primarily responsible for the flexion of the hip joint during walking?
A. Gluteus medius B. Iliopsoas (Primary hip flexor) C. Rectus femoris D. Tensor fasciae latae
Answer: B — Iliopsoas
Why? The iliopsoas is the primary and strongest flexor of the hip joint. It is formed by the iliacus and psoas major muscles. The rectus femoris and tensor fasciae latae are accessory hip flexors, but the iliopsoas is the main one.
High-Yield: The iliopsoas is innervated by the femoral nerve (L2-L4) and direct branches from the lumbar plexus. It is a common site for abscesses (psoas abscess) in TB or Crohn's disease.
Q18
Which structure is most affected by a rupture of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL)?
A. Medial meniscus B. Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) C. Tibia's anterior translation D. Fibula's posterior translation
Answer: C — Tibia's anterior translation
Why? The ACL prevents the tibia from sliding forward (anterior translation) relative to the femur. When it ruptures, the tibia can translate anteriorly. This is tested clinically with the Lachman test (most sensitive) and the anterior drawer test.
High-Yield: The "unhappy triad" of knee injuries involves the ACL, medial meniscus, and medial collateral ligament (MCL), often from a valgus force with a planted foot. PCL rupture causes posterior tibial translation (posterior drawer sign).
Q19
The majority of the blood supply to the anterior wall of the left ventricle is provided by which coronary artery?
A. Left circumflex artery B. Right coronary artery C. Left anterior descending artery D. Posterior descending artery
Answer: C — Left anterior descending artery
Why? The Left Anterior Descending (LAD) artery supplies the anterior wall of the left ventricle, the anterior two-thirds of the interventricular septum, and the apex. It is clinically the most critical coronary artery; occlusion causes a massive anterior myocardial infarction.
High-Yield: The LAD is known as the "widow-maker" because its sudden occlusion can be rapidly fatal. The right coronary artery (RCA) supplies the right ventricle and inferior wall. The circumflex supplies the lateral wall.
Q20
In patients with severe liver cirrhosis, which of the following is most likely to lead to ascites?
A. Decreased renal filtration B. Increased plasma oncotic pressure C. Decreased serum albumin production D. Increased lymphatic drainage
Answer: C — Decreased serum albumin production
Why? The liver produces albumin, which is the main contributor to plasma oncotic pressure. In cirrhosis, albumin production is impaired, leading to hypoalbuminemia. This decreases plasma oncotic pressure, causing fluid to leak out of the intravascular space into the peritoneal cavity, resulting in ascites. Other factors include portal hypertension and secondary hyperaldosteronism.
High-Yield: Ascites management: sodium restriction, diuretics (spironolactone ± furosemide), and therapeutic paracentesis. Refractory ascites may require TIPS (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt) or liver transplantation.
Q21
Which of the following is true regarding the renal countercurrent multiplier mechanism?
A. It helps concentrate urine by exchanging ions between the renal cortex and medulla B. It occurs primarily in the renal corpuscle C. It is mediated by the ascending limb of the loop of Henle D. It decreases the osmolarity of urine
Answer: C — It is mediated by the ascending limb of the loop of Henle
Why? The countercurrent multiplier system relies on the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, which is impermeable to water but actively pumps out sodium and chloride (Na+/K+/2Cl- cotransporter). This creates a hyperosmotic medullary interstitium, allowing water reabsorption in the descending limb and collecting ducts (under ADH influence) to concentrate urine.
High-Yield: The vasa recta act as countercurrent exchangers to maintain the medullary osmotic gradient. Loop diuretics (furosemide) inhibit the Na+/K+/2Cl- cotransporter in the ascending limb, destroying the gradient and causing diuresis.
Q22
Which of the following statements best describes the role of the pericardium in heart function?
A. It decreases friction between the heart and surrounding tissues B. It helps maintain the heart's shape during systole C. It generates electrical impulses to regulate heart rate D. It acts as a mechanical pump for blood circulation
Answer: A — It decreases friction between the heart and surrounding tissues
Why? The pericardium is a double-walled sac containing the heart and the roots of the great vessels. The inner (serous) layer secretes pericardial fluid, which acts as a lubricant to reduce friction between the heart and surrounding structures during contraction and relaxation. It also helps anchor the heart in the mediastinum and prevents overfilling.
High-Yield: The electrical impulse is generated by the SA node (not the pericardium). The heart's pumping action is by the myocardium. Cardiac tamponade (fluid accumulation in the pericardial sac) restricts diastolic filling and causes obstructive shock.
Q23
The primary function of surfactant in the lungs is to:
A. Increase the rate of gas exchange in the alveoli B. Reduce surface tension, preventing alveolar collapse C. Regulate blood flow to the lungs D. Enhance the exchange of oxygen with hemoglobin
Answer: B — Reduce surface tension, preventing alveolar collapse
Why? Surfactant is produced by Type II pneumocytes. It reduces alveolar surface tension, which prevents alveolar collapse at the end of expiration (atelectasis) and increases lung compliance, reducing the work of breathing. Lack of surfactant (e.g., in premature infants) causes Respiratory Distress Syndrome.
High-Yield: Surfactant is a mixture of lipids (mainly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, DPPC) and proteins. It is essential for maintaining alveolar stability. The Law of Laplace states that alveolar pressure is inversely proportional to radius; surfactant equalizes pressures between small and large alveoli.
Q24
In the case of a spinal cord injury at the level of C3, what is the most likely result?
A. Complete loss of sensation and movement below the level of injury B. Paralysis of the lower limbs with intact upper limb function C. Inability to breathe independently D. Inability to sense pain in the upper limbs
Answer: C — Inability to breathe independently
Why? The phrenic nerve, which innervates the diaphragm (the primary muscle of respiration), arises from spinal roots C3, C4, and C5 ("C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive"). An injury at C3 disrupts these lower motor neurons or the descending upper motor neurons supplying them, resulting in diaphragmatic paralysis and an inability to breathe independently. Patients require mechanical ventilation.
High-Yield: C1-C2 injuries: usually fatal without immediate respiratory support. C3-C4: ventilator-dependent. C5: some diaphragm function, may breathe on their own but with poor cough. High cervical injuries also cause neurogenic shock (hypotension and bradycardia).
Q25
Which of the following changes occurs during the fight-or-flight response, mediated by the sympathetic nervous system?
A. Increased peristalsis in the gastrointestinal tract B. Decreased heart rate C. Dilation of the bronchioles D. Constriction of the pupils
Answer: C — Dilation of the bronchioles
Why? The sympathetic nervous system mediates the "fight-or-flight" response. This includes bronchodilation (via β2-adrenergic receptors) to increase oxygen intake, tachycardia (increased heart rate), mydriasis (pupillary dilation), and decreased GI peristalsis (blood is shunted to skeletal muscles).
High-Yield: Sympathetic effects: "fight or flight" — increased HR, BP, respiratory rate, blood glucose, and pupil size; decreased digestion and salivation. Parasympathetic effects: "rest and digest" — opposite effects (e.g., bronchoconstriction, increased peristalsis, miosis).
Q26
The "Baroreceptor Reflex" helps to regulate blood pressure by primarily acting on which system?
A. Respiratory system B. Nervous system C. Renal system D. Immune system
Answer: B — Nervous system
Why? Baroreceptors are stretch-sensitive mechanoreceptors located in the carotid sinus and aortic arch. When blood pressure changes, they send signals via the glossopharyngeal (CN IX) and vagus (CN X) nerves to the brainstem (medulla). The medulla then adjusts sympathetic and parasympathetic outflow to the heart and blood vessels to restore normal blood pressure. Thus, the reflex acts primarily through the nervous system.
High-Yield: Baroreceptor reflex: a rapid, short-term regulator of BP. If BP falls, baroreceptor firing decreases → sympathetic outflow increases → vasoconstriction and tachycardia. If BP rises, baroreceptor firing increases → parasympathetic outflow increases → bradycardia and vasodilation.
Q27
A 35-year-old male presents after falling from a height. He has localized pain and swelling in his left forearm. The X-ray shows a break in the bone with a slight displacement. What type of fracture is depicted in the X-ray?
A. Spiral fracture B. Comminuted fracture C. Greenstick fracture D. Transverse fracture
Answer: D — Transverse fracture
Why? Based on the options and the description of a direct blow or fall causing a "break in the bone with slight displacement," a transverse fracture (a fracture straight across the bone) is the most likely. Spiral fractures occur from twisting forces. Comminuted fractures involve multiple bone fragments. Greenstick fractures are incomplete fractures seen in children.
High-Yield: Fracture descriptions: Transverse (perpendicular to bone axis), Oblique (angled), Spiral (twisting force), Comminuted (multiple fragments), Greenstick (incomplete, pediatric), Avulsion (tendon/ligament pulls bone fragment).
Q28
A 28-year-old female presents with severe shoulder pain after a car accident. She has limited range of motion in her left arm. The X-ray shows the humeral head displaced anteriorly. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Anterior shoulder dislocation B. Posterior shoulder dislocation C. Hill-Sachs lesion D. Rotator cuff tear
Answer: A — Anterior shoulder dislocation
Why? The humeral head being displaced anteriorly (and inferiorly) is the most common type of shoulder dislocation (95% of cases). It typically occurs from a blow to an abducted, externally rotated arm. Patients hold the arm in slight abduction and external rotation. A Hill-Sachs lesion is a cortical depression in the humeral head that occurs as a complication of anterior dislocation, not the dislocation itself.
High-Yield: Anterior dislocation: check for axillary nerve injury (deltoid weakness, numbness over lateral shoulder). Posterior dislocation: often caused by seizures or electrocution, presents with a "lightbulb" sign on AP X-ray and fixed internal rotation.
Q29
A 58-year-old man presents with chronic knee pain. His X-ray shows joint space narrowing and osteophyte formation. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Rheumatoid arthritis B. Osteoarthritis C. Gout D. Patellofemoral syndrome
Answer: B — Osteoarthritis
Why? Joint space narrowing and osteophyte (bone spur) formation are hallmark radiological features of osteoarthritis. Rheumatoid arthritis typically shows symmetric joint space narrowing, erosions, and osteopenia (without osteophytes initially). Gout shows "punched-out" erosions with overhanging edges.
High-Yield: Osteoarthritis: "wear and tear" arthritis, affects weight-bearing joints (knees, hips), pain worse with activity, relieved by rest. Treatment: weight loss, exercise, NSAIDs, intra-articular steroid injections, and joint replacement in end-stage disease.
Q30
A child presents with a noticeable limp and complaints of one leg being longer than the other. X-rays of both legs show asymmetric growth plates. What condition is most likely responsible for the discrepancy in leg lengths?
A. Developmental dysplasia of the hip B. Congenital scoliosis C. Legg-Calve-Perthes disease D. Epiphyseal injury
Answer: D — Epiphyseal injury
Why? An injury to the growth plate (epiphyseal plate) can result in premature closure or altered growth of that specific bone, leading to a limb length discrepancy. While Legg-Calve-Perthes (avascular necrosis of the femoral head) can cause a limp and shortening, the X-ray specifically mentions "asymmetric growth plates," pointing directly to an epiphyseal injury (Salter-Harris fracture).
High-Yield: Salter-Harris fractures (I-V) involve the growth plate. Type I: through the physis. Type II: through physis and metaphysis. Type III: through physis and epiphysis. Type IV: through metaphysis, physis, and epiphysis. Type V: crush injury. Types III-V have higher risk of growth disturbance.
Q31
A 42-year-old male presents with severe pelvic pain after a motor vehicle accident. CT imaging reveals a fracture of the acetabulum. Which type of fracture is most likely identified?
A. Acetabular fracture B. Pubic symphysis fracture C. Sacral fracture
Answer: A — Acetabular fracture
Why? The question directly states that the CT imaging reveals a fracture of the acetabulum. Therefore, the most likely identified fracture is an acetabular fracture. This is often a high-energy injury (e.g., dashboard impact in an MVA) and may be associated with hip dislocation or femoral head injury.
High-Yield: Acetabular fractures require careful assessment. They can be associated with sciatic nerve injury, femoral head fractures, and late post-traumatic osteoarthritis. Treatment may involve traction, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), or total hip arthroplasty in elderly patients.
Q32
A 65-year-old male with a history of prostate cancer presents with localized back pain. A bone scan shows multiple areas of increased uptake in the vertebrae. What is the most likely cause of these findings?
A. Osteoarthritis B. Osteomyelitis C. Metastatic bone disease D. Compression fractures
Answer: C — Metastatic bone disease
Why? Prostate cancer is well known for spreading to bones, particularly the vertebrae, pelvis, and ribs, via Batson's venous plexus. These metastases are typically osteoblastic (bone-forming), which appear as multiple areas of increased uptake ("hot spots") on a bone scan. Back pain in a patient with a history of prostate cancer is a red flag for metastasis.
High-Yield: Prostate cancer bone metastases are often osteoblastic (sclerotic). Breast cancer metastases are often mixed (lytic and blastic). Renal cell and thyroid cancers cause osteolytic lesions. Bone scan is sensitive for osteoblastic metastases.
Q33
A 72-year-old woman falls while walking and complains of pain in her hip. X-ray shows a displaced fracture at the neck of the femur. What is the most likely treatment option for this type of fracture?
A. Non-weight-bearing cast B. Hip replacement surgery C. Traction D. Intramedullary nailing
Answer: B — Hip replacement surgery
Why? A displaced femoral neck fracture in an elderly patient (>65 years) carries a high risk of avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head because the blood supply is disrupted. The definitive treatment is hip replacement surgery (hemiarthroplasty or total hip arthroplasty) to allow early mobilization and prevent complications of prolonged bed rest. Intramedullary nailing is used for intertrochanteric or subtrochanteric fractures, not displaced neck fractures.
High-Yield: Femoral neck fractures: intracapsular, high risk of AVN. In young patients, urgent internal fixation (cannulated screws) is done to preserve the femoral head. In elderly patients, arthroplasty is preferred. Intertrochanteric fractures: extracapsular, treated with sliding hip screw or cephalomedullary nail.
Q34
A 65-year-old man presents with back pain and leg weakness. MRI of the lumbar spine shows narrowing of the spinal canal with compression of nerve roots. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Herniated disc B. Spinal stenosis C. Spondylolisthesis D. Spinal tumor
Answer: B — Spinal stenosis
Why? Narrowing of the spinal canal with compression of nerve roots in an elderly patient is classic for lumbar spinal stenosis, often due to degenerative changes (osteoarthritis, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy). It classically causes neurogenic claudication (leg pain and weakness with walking or standing, relieved by sitting or leaning forward).
High-Yield: Spinal stenosis vs. vascular claudication: neurogenic claudication is relieved by bending forward (increases canal diameter) and is not related to exercise intensity. Vascular claudication is relieved by rest and is related to the amount of exercise. MRI is the imaging of choice.
Q35
A 20-year-old athlete presents with a painful, swollen ankle after an inversion injury. X-ray shows an avulsion fracture at the lateral malleolus. What is the most likely cause of this type of fracture?
A. Anterior talofibular ligament injury
Answer: A — Anterior talofibular ligament injury
Why? An inversion injury of the ankle stretches the lateral ligaments. The most commonly injured ligament is the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL). In severe cases, the force can pull off a small piece of bone from the lateral malleolus where the ligament attaches, causing an avulsion fracture.
High-Yield: The Ottawa Ankle Rules help determine the need for X-rays after ankle injury (pain in malleolar zone, tenderness at posterior edge of lateral malleolus, inability to bear weight 4 steps). Most avulsion fractures are managed conservatively with a walking boot or cast.
Q36
A 16-year-old girl presents with a noticeable curvature of her spine. X-ray shows a lateral curvature greater than 25 degrees. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Kyphosis B. Scoliosis C. Lordosis D. Scheuermann's disease
Answer: B — Scoliosis
Why? Scoliosis is defined as a lateral curvature of the spine >10 degrees (measured by the Cobb angle). A curvature >25 degrees in an adolescent requires close monitoring and possibly bracing (if 25-40 degrees and skeletally immature) or surgery (if >40-50 degrees). Kyphosis is an excessive backward curvature (hunchback). Lordosis is an excessive inward curvature.
High-Yield: Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is the most common type. The Adams forward bend test is used for screening. Treatment: observation for <25°, bracing for 25–40°, and spinal fusion for >40–50° or progressive curves.
Q37
A 60-year-old woman falls onto an outstretched hand. X-ray shows a distal radius fracture with dorsal angulation. What is the most likely type of fracture seen here?
A. Colles' fracture B. Smith fracture C. Barton fracture D. Monteggia fracture
Answer: A — Colles' fracture
Why? A Colles' fracture is a distal radius fracture with dorsal displacement/angulation, typically caused by a fall onto an outstretched hand (FOOSH). It is very common in elderly osteoporotic women. A Smith fracture has volar (palmar) displacement. A Barton fracture involves the articular surface with dislocation. A Monteggia fracture involves the proximal ulna with radial head dislocation.
High-Yield: Colles' fracture classically presents with a "dinner fork" deformity. It may be associated with median nerve injury (carpal tunnel syndrome) and extensor pollicis longus tendon rupture. Treatment: closed reduction and casting, or surgical fixation if unstable.
Q38
A 14-year-old boy presents with pain and swelling in the right femur. X-ray shows an aggressive bone lesion with periosteal reaction. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Osteosarcoma B. Ewing's sarcoma C. Giant cell tumor D. Chondrosarcoma
Answer: B — Ewing's sarcoma
Why? Ewing's sarcoma typically affects children and adolescents (10–20 years) and occurs in the diaphysis (shaft) of long bones like the femur. It is an aggressive tumor that produces a classic "onion-skin" periosteal reaction on X-ray. Osteosarcoma also affects teenagers but usually occurs in the metaphysis (around the knee) and shows a "sunburst" periosteal reaction and Codman's triangle.
High-Yield: Ewing's sarcoma is a small round blue cell tumor, associated with a t(11;22) translocation. It is highly aggressive but responds well to chemotherapy + surgery/radiation. Osteosarcoma is the most common primary bone cancer, often associated with RB1 or TP53 mutations (Li-Fraumeni syndrome).
Q39
A 40-year-old man presents with heel pain after landing from a height. X-ray shows a comminuted fracture of the calcaneus. What is the most appropriate management for this injury?
A. Non-weight-bearing cast B. Surgical fixation C. Corticosteroid injections D. Physical therapy
Answer: B — Surgical fixation
Why? Comminuted intra-articular calcaneal fractures (often from a fall from a height) frequently require surgical fixation (ORIF) to restore the anatomy of the subtalar joint, prevent post-traumatic arthritis, and restore the height and width of the heel. Non-operative treatment may be considered for non-displaced or extra-articular fractures, or in patients with severe comorbidities.
High-Yield: Calcaneal fractures: check for associated lumbar spine fractures (as axial loading can cause both). Assess Böhler's angle on X-ray. Complications: compartment syndrome of the foot, subtalar arthritis, heel widening, and peroneal tendon impingement.
Q40
A 25-year-old long-distance runner presents with localized pain in the lower leg. MRI shows a hairline fracture in the distal tibia. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Stress fracture B. Tendonitis C. Osteomyelitis D. Sprain
Answer: A — Stress fracture
Why? A stress fracture is a small crack or severe bruising within a bone caused by repetitive mechanical stress, common in runners and athletes. MRI is very sensitive and can detect early stress reactions (bone marrow edema) and hairline fractures before they are visible on plain X-ray.
High-Yield: Common sites for stress fractures: tibia, metatarsals (march fracture), navicular, and femoral neck. Treatment: rest, modification of activity, and sometimes immobilization. Femoral neck stress fractures are high-risk and may require surgery.
Q41
A 50-year-old male with a history of hyperuricemia presents with acute pain and swelling in his big toe. X-ray shows tophi in the joint. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Rheumatoid arthritis B. Gouty arthritis C. Osteoarthritis D. Psoriatic arthritis
Answer: B — Gouty arthritis
Why? Acute monoarthritis of the first metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra) in a patient with hyperuricemia is classic for gouty arthritis. Tophi are deposits of monosodium urate crystals and appear on X-ray as soft tissue swellings or "punched-out" bony erosions with overhanging edges.
High-Yield: Gout is caused by hyperuricemia (often due to renal underexcretion or high purine diet). Acute attack treatment: NSAIDs, colchicine, or corticosteroids. Long-term management: allopurinol or febuxostat to lower uric acid. Avoid aspirin and diuretics.
Q42
Which of the following X-rays is used to detect kidney stones?
A. Retrograde Pyelogram B. Intravenous Pyelogram (IVP) C. Kidney, Ureter, and Bladder X-ray (KUB) D. Voiding Cystourethrogram (VCUG)
Answer: C — Kidney, Ureter, and Bladder X-ray (KUB)
Why? A KUB (plain abdominal X-ray) is the initial screening test for suspected kidney stones. It can detect radiopaque stones (calcium stones) but may miss radiolucent stones (uric acid). However, a non-contrast CT scan is now the gold standard for detecting stones, as it is more sensitive.
High-Yield: Most kidney stones are calcium oxalate (radiopaque). Uric acid stones are radiolucent (not visible on plain X-ray). CT KUB (non-contrast) is the investigation of choice for renal colic.
Q43
Which imaging technique involves injecting contrast into the urinary system to visualize the kidneys, ureters, and bladder?
A. Renal Arteriogram B. Intravenous Pyelogram (IVP) C. Cystogram D. Urethrogram
Answer: B — Intravenous Pyelogram (IVP)
Why? An Intravenous Pyelogram (IVP) (also called intravenous urogram, IVU) involves injecting a radiopaque contrast medium into a vein. The kidneys filter the contrast, which then passes through the ureters into the bladder, allowing visualization of the entire urinary tract.
High-Yield: IVP is useful for assessing structural abnormalities, obstructions, and tumors. However, CT urography has largely replaced IVP in many centers due to better resolution and sensitivity. IVP is contraindicated in renal failure and pregnancy.
Q44
What is the primary purpose of a Cystogram?
A. To visualize kidney stones B. To evaluate urinary reflux and bladder abnormalities C. To detect prostate cancer D. To measure kidney function
Answer: B — To evaluate urinary reflux and bladder abnormalities
Why? A cystogram involves injecting contrast directly into the bladder via a catheter. It is primarily used to evaluate bladder shape, capacity, and integrity (e.g., to detect leaks after trauma or surgery). A voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) is used to detect vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) and assess the urethra during voiding.
High-Yield: Cystogram indications: bladder trauma (to rule out rupture), recurrent UTIs in children (to rule out VUR), and postoperative assessment of bladder repair. It does not evaluate the kidneys directly.
Q45
Which of the following X-ray techniques is primarily used to visualize blood vessels of the kidney?
A. CT Scan of the Pelvis B. Retrograde Pyelogram C. Renal Arteriogram D. Urethrogram
Answer: C — Renal Arteriogram
Why? A renal arteriogram (or renal angiography) involves injecting contrast directly into the renal artery (usually via a catheter in the femoral artery) to visualize the blood vessels of the kidney. It is used to diagnose renal artery stenosis, aneurysms, and to guide interventions like embolization or angioplasty.
High-Yield: CT angiography (CTA) or MR angiography (MRA) are non-invasive alternatives to conventional renal arteriography. Renal arteriography is still the gold standard for diagnosing renal artery stenosis and is therapeutic (e.g., stenting) in the same session.
Q46
A CT Urogram is used to:
A. Visualize the blood vessels in the kidney B. Identify tumors, stones, or structural defects in the urinary system C. Detect urinary reflux D. Examine the bladder during voiding
Answer: B — Identify tumors, stones, or structural defects in the urinary system
Why? A CT Urogram (CTU) involves intravenous contrast and scanning in multiple phases to visualize the kidneys, ureters, and bladder. It is highly sensitive for detecting stones, tumors (e.g., transitional cell carcinoma), and structural abnormalities of the urinary tract. It has largely replaced IVP for these indications.
High-Yield: CT Urogram is the gold standard for hematuria evaluation (to rule out urothelial carcinoma). It requires adequate renal function because the contrast is nephrotoxic. Non-contrast CT KUB is used for acute stone disease.
Q47
Which X-ray is used to assess the urethra for strictures or tears, often after trauma?
A. Voiding Cystourethrogram (VCUG) B. Retrograde Urethrogram C. Intravenous Pyelogram (IVP) D. Renal Arteriogram
Answer: A — Voiding Cystourethrogram (VCUG) / Retrograde Urethrogram
Why? A retrograde urethrogram (RUG) is specifically used to assess the anterior urethra for strictures or tears (e.g., after straddle injury or pelvic trauma). A voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) is used to assess the posterior urethra and bladder during voiding. Both are used for urethral evaluation.
High-Yield: In suspected urethral injury (e.g., blood at meatus, high-riding prostate, pelvic fracture), a retrograde urethrogram must be performed BEFORE attempting catheterization to avoid worsening the injury. A "cortex" or "rosebud" appearance may indicate a stricture.
Q48
A Renal Arteriogram is used to diagnose which of the following conditions?
A. Kidney stones B. Urinary tract infection C. Aneurysms or stenosis in kidney blood vessels D. Bladder cancer
Answer: C — Aneurysms or stenosis in kidney blood vessels
Why? As the name suggests, a renal arteriogram visualizes the arteries of the kidney. It is used to diagnose vascular abnormalities such as renal artery stenosis (a cause of secondary hypertension), renal artery aneurysms, and arteriovenous malformations.
High-Yield: Renal artery stenosis is a common cause of secondary hypertension, especially in older patients with atherosclerosis or younger women with fibromuscular dysplasia. It can lead to ischemic nephropathy and flash pulmonary edema.
Q49
Which imaging technique is most useful for detecting tumors or structural defects in the pelvic region, such as in the bladder or prostate?
A. CT Scan of the Pelvis B. Retrograde Pyelogram C. Urethrogram D. Intravenous Pyelogram (IVP)
Answer: A — CT Scan of the Pelvis
Why? A CT scan of the pelvis provides high-resolution cross-sectional images of all pelvic organs, including the bladder, prostate, uterus, and rectum. It is excellent for detecting tumors, structural defects, lymphadenopathy, and metastases. It is the preferred imaging modality for staging pelvic malignancies.
High-Yield: For prostate cancer, MRI (multiparametric MRI) is superior for local staging. For bladder cancer, CT urography is used for staging and surveillance. Transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) is used for prostate biopsy guidance.
Q50
What is the gold standard diagnostic test for diagnosing a perforated peptic ulcer?
A. Ultrasound B. CT scan with oral contrast C. Endoscopy D. Plain X-ray
Answer: D — Plain X-ray (Erect CXR)
Why? An erect chest X-ray (or erect abdominal X-ray) is the initial and most readily available test for a perforated peptic ulcer. It looks for free air under the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum), which is present in about 70-80% of cases. While CT is more sensitive, plain X-ray is the classic "gold standard" initial step in a resource-limited or acute setting. Endoscopy is contraindicated if perforation is suspected (risk of worsening the leak).
High-Yield: If plain X-ray is negative but clinical suspicion is high, a CT scan (with oral/IV contrast) is the next step. Treatment: resuscitation, IV antibiotics, and urgent surgery (omental patch repair).
Q51
What is the main complication of an ileostomy, and how can it be prevented?
A. Dehydration and electrolyte imbalance; by careful fluid management B. Infection; by using prophylactic antibiotics C. Stoma prolapse; by proper stoma care D. Skin irritation; by avoiding tight-fitting stoma bags
Answer: A — Dehydration and electrolyte imbalance; by careful fluid management
Why? An ileostomy bypasses the colon, which is responsible for absorbing water and electrolytes. This leads to high-output liquid stools, putting the patient at significant risk of dehydration and electrolyte imbalance (especially sodium and potassium). Patients must be educated to drink adequate fluids and monitor their output. Skin irritation and prolapse are common complications but not the main systemic complication.
High-Yield: High-output ileostomy (>1.5 L/day) requires careful management with oral rehydration solutions (e.g., St. Mark's solution), anti-motility agents (loperamide), and sometimes IV fluids. Avoid high-fibre foods and sugary drinks.
Q52
Which type of abdominal incision is preferred for a midline laparotomy in trauma patients, and why?
A. McBurney's incision, for quicker access B. Midline incision, because it allows for better access to both upper and lower abdomen C. Pfannenstiel incision, for cosmetic reasons D. Subcostal incision, to avoid muscle damage
Answer: B — Midline incision, because it allows for better access to both upper and lower abdomen
Why? In trauma, the midline incision is preferred because it is quick to make, provides excellent access to almost all intra-abdominal organs (both upper and lower abdomen), and can be rapidly extended if necessary. It is also relatively avascular. McBurney's is for appendicitis, Pfannenstiel is for gynecological/obstetric procedures, and subcostal is for biliary surgery.
High-Yield: The midline incision goes through the linea alba (avascular). Complications include incisional hernia, dehiscence, and wound infection. Mass closure with non-absorbable sutures is standard.

Q58
How do you manage a patient with a strangulated femoral hernia?
A. Wait for it to resolve spontaneously B. Administer analgesia and antibiotics, then observe C. Immediate surgery to reduce and repair the hernia D. Apply ice packs to reduce swelling before surgical consultation
Answer: C — Immediate surgery to reduce and repair the hernia
Why? A strangulated hernia is a surgical emergency. The blood supply to the herniated bowel is compromised, and if not relieved urgently, it will become ischemic and perforate. The patient needs immediate surgery to release the constriction, inspect the bowel, and resect if non-viable. Femoral hernias have a particularly high risk of strangulation due to the narrow, rigid femoral ring.
High-Yield: Strangulated hernia signs: severe pain, tenderness, fever, tachycardia, and signs of obstruction (vomiting, distension). Do not attempt manual reduction if strangulation is suspected (risk of reducing ischemic bowel into the abdomen).
Q59
What is the difference between a direct and indirect inguinal hernia, and what is their clinical significance in terms of complications?
A. Direct hernias pass through the inguinal canal; indirect hernias are medial to the inferior epigastric vessels B. Direct hernias occur laterally to the inferior epigastric vessels; indirect hernias pass through the inguinal canal C. Indirect hernias are less likely to incarcerate or strangulate D. Direct hernias are less prone to complications than indirect hernias

Why? Correction: The classic anatomical definition is that indirect hernias pass through the deep inguinal ring and inguinal canal, lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels. Direct hernias protrude through Hesselbach's triangle, medial to the inferior epigastric vessels. Option B in the provided exam paper has a wording error (it says direct is lateral and indirect passes through the canal). Q60
In the context of a strangulated hernia, what is the initial step in management before surgery?
A. Administering prophylactic antibiotics B. Resuscitation with IV fluids and NG tube decompression C. Immediate manual reduction D. Applying a truss
Answer: A — Administering prophylactic antibiotics (and resuscitation)
Why? The initial step in managing a strangulated hernia is resuscitation (IV fluids, NG tube if obstructed) and administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics to cover the risk of bowel ischemia and bacterial translocation. Manual reduction is contraindicated if strangulation is suspected. Surgery must follow promptly.
High-Yield: Antibiotics should be given before surgery to reduce the risk of wound infection and sepsis, especially if bowel resection is required. Analgesia should also be given.
Q61
What is the most common complication of inguinal hernia repair?
A. Recurrence of the hernia B. Chronic pain C. Hemorrhage D. Infection
Answer: B — Chronic pain (chronic groin pain)
Why? Chronic groin pain (inguinodynia) is the most common long-term complication after inguinal hernia repair, affecting up to 10–15% of patients. It is caused by nerve entrapment or irritation (ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric, or genitofemoral nerves). Recurrence is less common now due to mesh techniques but still occurs.
High-Yield: Prevention of chronic pain: careful nerve identification and preservation, use of lightweight mesh, and atraumatic dissection. Treatment: nerve blocks, neuromodulation, or surgical neurectomy.
Q62
Describe the features of a sliding hernia and its surgical significance.
A. It contains a portion of the stomach, making repair more difficult B. It involves a portion of an organ in the hernia sac, and repair must avoid injury to the organ C. It is a congenital hernia that resolves on its own D. It is a type of hernia that only affects elderly individuals
Answer: B — It involves a portion of an organ in the hernia sac, and repair must avoid injury to the organ
Why? A sliding hernia (e.g., sliding hiatal hernia or sliding inguinal hernia) occurs when a portion of an organ (e.g., stomach, colon, or bladder) forms part of the wall of the hernia sac, rather than just being contained within it. During surgical repair, the surgeon must be extremely careful to avoid injury to the organ and to properly reduce it.
High-Yield: Sliding hiatal hernias are common (type I). Sliding inguinal hernias often involve the sigmoid colon or cecum. Repair requires mobilizing the organ and closing the defect (e.g., cruroplasty for hiatal hernia).
Q63
How does one differentiate between a pressure ulcer and a non-pressure-related skin ulcer?
A. Pressure ulcers are more common in elderly patients B. Pressure ulcers occur over bony prominences with a history of prolonged pressure; non-pressure ulcers have different etiologies like arterial insufficiency C. Pressure ulcers are always painful, while non-pressure ulcers are painless D. Non-pressure ulcers are more common in patients with diabetes
Answer: B — Pressure ulcers occur over bony prominences with a history of prolonged pressure; non-pressure ulcers have different etiologies like arterial insufficiency
Why? The defining feature of a pressure ulcer (bed sore) is its location over a bony prominence (sacrum, heels, greater trochanter) in a patient with a history of immobility and prolonged pressure. Non-pressure ulcers (e.g., arterial, venous, diabetic, neuropathic) occur in different locations and have different underlying causes.
High-Yield: Pressure ulcer staging (I–IV): Stage I = non-blanchable erythema; Stage II = partial-thickness skin loss; Stage III = full-thickness skin loss; Stage IV = full-thickness tissue loss with exposed bone/tendon. Prevention: repositioning, pressure-relieving mattresses, and skin care.
Q64
What are the key clinical signs of septic shock, and how are they differentiated from hypovolemic shock?
A. Fever, tachycardia, hypotension, and altered mental status; septic shock has fever and vasodilation (warm extremities) B. Decreased heart rate, hypotension, and cyanosis; hypovolemic shock presents with fever C. Both present identically; only lab tests can differentiate D. Septic shock always has cold extremities, while hypovolemic shock has warm extremities
Answer: A — Fever, tachycardia, hypotension, altered mental status; septic shock has vasodilation
Why? Septic shock is a form of distributive shock caused by severe infection. It leads to massive vasodilation and increased capillary permeability, presenting with fever (often), tachycardia, hypotension, and warm, flushed skin (early phase). Hypovolemic shock is caused by fluid loss and presents with cold, clammy skin, delayed capillary refill, and no fever (unless the cause is infected).
High-Yield: Septic shock: warm extremities (early), bounding pulses, low SVR. Hypovolemic shock: cold extremities, thready pulses, high SVR. Both have tachycardia and hypotension. Lactate is elevated in both but often higher in septic shock.
Q65
What is the common cause of sepsis in burn patients and how should it be treated?
A. Pseudomonas aeruginosa; with debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics B. Escherichia coli; with oral antibiotics C. Staphylococcus aureus; with local wound care D. Clostridium perfringens; with hyperbaric oxygen therapy
Answer: A — Pseudomonas aeruginosa; with debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics
Why? Burn wounds are initially sterile but quickly become colonized by skin flora and hospital-acquired organisms. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a classic and dangerous cause of burn wound sepsis, often leading to invasive infection and death. Treatment requires early excision and debridement of necrotic tissue, topical antimicrobials (e.g., silver sulfadiazine), and systemic broad-spectrum antibiotics. Gram-positive organisms like MRSA are also common.
High-Yield: Burn sepsis is the leading cause of death in major burns. The burn wound is the primary source. Prevention: early excision and grafting, strict infection control, and topical antimicrobials. Empiric antibiotics should cover P. aeruginosa and S. aureus.
