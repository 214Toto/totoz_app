Multiple Choice Questions & Clinical Scenarios
Q1
Which of the following is the correct sequence of the phases of healing?
A. Remodeling, hemostasis, inflammation, maturation B. Hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, maturation C. Proliferation, inflammation, maturation, remodeling D. Inflammation, proliferation, remodeling, maturation E. Inflammation, hemostasis, proliferation, maturation
Answer: B — Hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, maturation
Why? Wound healing occurs in four distinct, overlapping phases: 1) Hemostasis (immediate; clot formation), 2) Inflammation (days 1-4; neutrophils then macrophages), 3) Proliferation (days 4-21; fibroblasts, collagen, angiogenesis, granulation tissue), and 4) Maturation/Remodeling (day 21 to 1 year; collagen cross-linking and remodeling).
High-Yield: Healing by primary intention (clean surgical wounds) vs secondary intention (infected or large wounds with granulation tissue). The maturation phase is the longest and determines the final tensile strength (max 80% of original).
Q2
Which are the first infiltrating cells to enter the wound?
A. T-lymphocytes B. Macrophages C. Neutrophils D. Monocytes E. Fibroblasts
Answer: C — Neutrophils
Why? Neutrophils are the first inflammatory cells to arrive at the wound site (within minutes to hours). They are followed by macrophages (which peak at 48–72 hours), then lymphocytes. Neutrophils primarily function to phagocytose bacteria and debris.
High-Yield: Macrophages are the "key orchestrators" of healing; they release growth factors (PDGF, TGF-β) that recruit fibroblasts and stimulate angiogenesis. Fibroblasts appear later to synthesize collagen.
Q3
Steroids impair wound healing by:
A. Decreasing neutrophil and macrophage migration B. Increasing release of lysosomal enzymes C. Increasing collagen lysis D. Decreasing platelet plug integrity
Answer: A — Decreasing neutrophil and macrophage migration
Why? Glucocorticoids (steroids) are potent anti-inflammatory agents. They impair wound healing primarily by inhibiting the migration of neutrophils and macrophages to the wound site, thereby suppressing the inflammatory phase. They also inhibit fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis.
High-Yield: Other factors delaying wound healing: diabetes, malnutrition, vitamin C deficiency (impaired collagen cross-linking), zinc deficiency, ischaemia, infection, smoking, and chemotherapy/radiotherapy.
Q4
A pressure ulcer that extends through the subcutaneous tissue in which fascia may be visible but is not violated is what stage?
A. Stage II B. Stage IV C. Stage III D. Stage I
Answer: C — Stage III
Why? Pressure ulcer staging: Stage I = non-blanchable erythema of intact skin; Stage II = partial-thickness skin loss (epidermis/dermis); Stage III = full-thickness skin loss extending into subcutaneous tissue (fascia may be visible but not violated); Stage IV = full-thickness tissue loss with exposed bone, tendon, or muscle.
High-Yield: Prevention of pressure ulcers includes frequent repositioning (every 2 hours), pressure-relieving mattresses, adequate nutrition, and skin care. Common sites: sacrum, heels, ischial tuberosities.
Q5
Which of the following individuals is likely to have the greatest total body water?
A. 43-year-old female who has a sedentary office job B. 29-year-old obese computer programmer C. A 94-year-old man who is partially dependent in daily activities D. A 22-year-old male university rugby player
Answer: D — A 22-year-old male university rugby player
Why? Total body water (TBW) as a percentage of body weight is highest in young, lean, muscular males (approx. 60-65%). Fat tissue contains very little water (about 10-20%), while muscle is rich in water (about 75%). Therefore, a young, athletic male has a higher proportion of lean muscle mass and a lower proportion of body fat, giving him the greatest total body water. TBW decreases with age and is lower in females and obese individuals.
High-Yield: TBW = 60% of body weight in adult males, 50-55% in adult females, and 70-80% in neonates. Intracellular fluid (ICF) is 2/3 of TBW, and extracellular fluid (ECF) is 1/3.
Q6
On average, which of the following organs produces the smallest volume of fluid in the body?
A. Ileum B. Pancreas C. Salivary glands D. Stomach
Answer: C — Salivary glands
Why? Daily fluid production in the GI tract: Saliva ~1.5 L/day, Stomach (gastric juice) ~2 L/day, Pancreas (pancreatic juice) ~1.5-2 L/day, Liver (bile) ~0.5-1 L/day, Small intestine (including ileum) ~2-3 L/day. The salivary glands produce the smallest volume among the major GI secretory organs listed.
High-Yield: Massive fluid losses can occur from vomiting (gastric acid → hypochloremic hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis), diarrhea (pancreatic/biliary fluid → metabolic acidosis), or high-output fistulas.

Q8
Which of the following shows the correct order of intravenous fluids from least to greatest intravascular volume expansion?
A. D5W, 0.45% normal saline, 5% albumin, 0.9% normal saline B. Ringers lactate, 5% albumin, 0.45% normal saline, D5W C. D5W, Ringers lactate, 0.45% normal saline, 0.9% normal saline D. 0.45% normal saline, Ringers lactate, D5W, 5% albumin E. D5W, 0.45% normal saline, Ringers lactate, 5% albumin
Answer: E — D5W, 0.45% normal saline, Ringers lactate, 5% albumin
Why? Intravascular volume expansion depends on the fluid's tonicity and colloid content:
1) D5W: Dextrose is metabolized, leaving free water that distributes across all body compartments. It provides the least intravascular expansion (about 1/12 of the infused volume stays intravascular).
2) 0.45% normal saline (half-normal): Hypotonic crystalloid; distributes mostly into ICF and ECF, with some intravascular expansion.
3) Ringers lactate: Isotonic crystalloid; distributes into ECF, with ~25% remaining intravascular.
4) 5% albumin: Colloid; remains almost entirely intravascular (expands by ~100% of infused volume).
Therefore, the order from least to greatest is D5W < 0.45% NS < Ringers lactate < 5% albumin.
High-Yield: Colloids (albumin, dextran) expand intravascular volume more than crystalloids, but they are more expensive and do not improve mortality compared to crystalloids. Crystalloids are first-line for resuscitation.
Q9
Which statement is true regarding crystalloid fluids?
A. 0.9% normal saline is isotonic whereas Ringers lactate is hypotonic relative to plasma B. 0.9% normal saline contains 145 mEq/L of sodium and 145 mEq/L of chloride C. Crystalloids are fluids composed of sterile water and some electrolytes, typically sodium and chloride D. The molecules contained in crystalloid fluids are unable to cross the semipermeable vascular membrane under normal circumstances E. Ringers lactate is a crystalloid fluid that contains potassium, calcium and bicarbonate
Answer: C — Crystalloids are fluids composed of sterile water and some electrolytes, typically sodium and chloride
Why? Crystalloids are solutions of small water-soluble molecules (electrolytes) that can freely cross the semipermeable capillary membrane. The defining characteristic is that they are sterile water with electrolytes (mainly sodium and chloride).
Correction of other options: A is false (Ringers is isotonic). B is false (normal saline has 154 mEq/L of Na and Cl). D is false (crystalloid molecules can cross the membrane; colloids cannot). E is partially false (Ringers lactate contains lactate, not bicarbonate).
High-Yield: Normal saline (0.9% NaCl): Na 154, Cl 154, pH 5.5 (can cause hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis). Ringers lactate: Na 130, K 4, Ca 3, Cl 109, Lactate 28 (metabolized to bicarbonate in the liver).
Q10
SIRS is defined clinically by which of the following 4 findings:
A. Leukocytosis, hypotension, tachycardia, fever B. Tachycardia, tachypnea, fever, hypotension C. Tachypnea, fever, leukocytosis, hypotension D. Tachycardia, tachypnea, fever, leukocytosis
Answer: D — Tachycardia, tachypnea, fever, leukocytosis
Why? The Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) criteria are: 1) Temperature >38°C or <36°C; 2) Heart rate >90/min; 3) Respiratory rate >20/min or PaCO2 <32 mmHg; 4) White blood cell count >12,000/mm³ or <4,000/mm³ or >10% band forms. Hypotension is not part of the SIRS definition; it indicates progression to sepsis or septic shock.
High-Yield: Sepsis = SIRS + suspected/documented infection. Septic shock = sepsis + hypotension despite adequate fluid resuscitation. The qSOFA score (respiratory rate ≥22, altered mentation, systolic BP ≤100) is used for rapid bedside screening.
Q11 & 13
A patient who withdraws from pain, is mumbling inappropriate words, and opens his eyes to pain, has a GCS score of:
A. 12 B. 9 C. 5 D. 3
Answer: B — 9 (Note: The exam paper lists D as the correct option, but the calculation yields 9. Let's calculate: Eye opening to pain = 2. Verbal response: inappropriate words = 3. Motor response: withdraws from pain = 4. Total = 2+3+4 = 9. The correct answer is B, not D. The provided answer key in the document may have an error.)
Why? Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scoring:
Eye Opening (E): Spontaneous = 4, To speech = 3, To pain = 2, None = 1.
Verbal Response (V): Oriented = 5, Confused = 4, Inappropriate words = 3, Incomprehensible sounds = 2, None = 1.
Motor Response (M): Obeys commands = 6, Localizes pain = 5, Withdraws from pain = 4, Abnormal flexion (decorticate) = 3, Extension (decerebrate) = 2, None = 1.
Total = 2 (E) + 3 (V) + 4 (M) = 9. The minimum GCS is 3, and the maximum is 15.
High-Yield: GCS ≤ 8 is a severe head injury and is an indication for definitive airway (intubation). GCS 9-12 is moderate, and GCS 13-15 is mild.
Q12
A definitive airway is indicated in patients with: (Select all that apply.)
A. Oropharyngeal soft tissue swelling B. GCS < 8 C. Apnea D. Severe Inhalational burns
Answer: A, B, C, D — All of the above
Why? A definitive airway (endotracheal intubation or surgical airway) is required when the patient cannot maintain a patent airway, cannot protect the airway, or cannot oxygenate/ventilate adequately. Indications include: apnea, GCS < 8 (unconscious, unable to protect airway), severe inhalational burns (risk of rapid airway oedema), oropharyngeal soft tissue swelling (e.g., Ludwig's angina, trauma), and maxillofacial trauma.
High-Yield: In trauma, the primary survey follows the ABCDE approach: Airway with cervical spine protection, Breathing, Circulation, Disability (GCS), and Exposure. Airway is always the first priority.
Q14
Regarding open pneumothorax, which of the following is NOT true:
A. Involves full thickness injury to the chest wall B. The wound should be temporally covered with a sterile gauze tapered on all four sides C. For permanent management, the chest wall defect should be closed, and a tube thoracostomy placed remote from the wound D. Symptoms may include hypoxia and tachypnea
Answer: B — The wound should be temporally covered with a sterile gauze tapered on all four sides
Why? An open pneumothorax (sucking chest wound) occurs when there is a full-thickness defect in the chest wall. Air enters the pleural space through the wound, causing lung collapse and hypoxia. The initial management is to apply a three-sided occlusive dressing (tape on three sides only, leaving one side open). This acts as a flutter valve, allowing air to escape during expiration but preventing air from entering during inspiration. Taping all four sides can convert an open pneumothorax into a life-threatening tension pneumothorax. Therefore, B is the false statement.
High-Yield: Definitive management of open pneumothorax: chest wall defect closure and chest tube insertion (tube thoracostomy) at a site remote from the wound. Never tape all four sides of the dressing.
Q15
A massive hemothorax is defined as: (Select all that apply.)
A. Volume of blood >3 L in the pleural space in adults B. Volume of blood >1500 mL in pleural space in adults C. Haemothorax visible on ultrasound D. In paediatric population, >25% of the patient's blood volume in the pleural space E. Haemothorax that is visible on radiograph
Answer: B and D
Why? A massive haemothorax is defined as the accumulation of >1500 mL of blood in the pleural cavity in adults, or >25% of the patient's blood volume in the paediatric population. It is a life-threatening emergency requiring simultaneous resuscitation and chest tube insertion. If >1500 mL is drained immediately or >200 mL/hour for 2-4 hours, urgent thoracotomy is indicated.
High-Yield: Massive haemothorax presents with shock, decreased breath sounds, and dull percussion on the affected side. FAST or CXR can detect it, but the definition is based on the volume of blood. Chest tube drainage is both diagnostic and therapeutic.
Q16
In trauma patients, altered mental status is the most common indication for:
A. Tracheostomy B. Intubation C. Application of a C-collar D. Admission to hospital
Answer: B — Intubation
Why? In a trauma patient, altered mental status (GCS ≤ 8) indicates that the patient cannot adequately protect their airway, increasing the risk of aspiration and hypoxia. The most common and critical indication for endotracheal intubation (definitive airway) in trauma is an altered mental status.
High-Yield: A definitive airway includes an endotracheal tube (cuffed and secured), or a surgical airway (cricothyroidotomy or tracheostomy). It is indicated for apnea, GCS < 8, severe maxillofacial trauma, and airway burns.
Q17
Which of the following is NOT appropriate to help determine the cause of hemorrhagic shock in a trauma patient?
A. CXR B. Abdominal CT scan C. Pelvic radiograph D. FAST E. E-FAST
Answer: B — Abdominal CT scan
Why? In a trauma patient with hemorrhagic shock (hemodynamically unstable), the patient is too unstable to be transported to the CT scanner. The source of bleeding must be identified rapidly at the bedside. The appropriate initial investigations for hemorrhagic shock include FAST (focused assessment with sonography for trauma), CXR (for hemothorax), and pelvic radiograph (for pelvic fractures). An abdominal CT scan is only appropriate for hemodynamically stable patients.
High-Yield: The "lethal triad" of trauma: hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy. The primary survey aims to identify and treat life-threatening injuries (e.g., massive hemothorax, cardiac tamponade, pelvic fracture) before proceeding to CT.
Q18
The overall goal of the primary survey is to:
A. Identify all traumatic injuries that the patient has B. Identify and treat life-threatening conditions C. Identify limb-threatening injuries D. Complete a thorough head-to-toe examination
Answer: B — Identify and treat life-threatening conditions
Why? The primary survey in trauma (ABCDE approach) is designed to rapidly identify and treat immediately life-threatening conditions (e.g., airway obstruction, tension pneumothorax, massive hemorrhage, cardiac tamponade). A thorough head-to-toe examination to identify all injuries is part of the secondary survey.
High-Yield: Primary survey: Airway with C-spine control, Breathing, Circulation with hemorrhage control, Disability (neurological), and Exposure with environmental control. Secondary survey: AMPLE history and head-to-toe examination.
Q19
Signs of tension pneumothorax include all of the following EXCEPT:
A. Decreased mental status B. Subcutaneous emphysema C. Hypotension D. Absent or decreased breath sounds
Answer: B — Subcutaneous emphysema
Why? Tension pneumothorax is a life-threatening emergency. Signs include hypotension, tachycardia, distended neck veins, tracheal deviation (away from the affected side), absent/decreased breath sounds, and hypoxia. Subcutaneous emphysema (air in the subcutaneous tissue) can occur with any pneumothorax (especially after rib fractures or chest trauma) but is not a defining feature or sign of tension pneumothorax.
High-Yield: Do not wait for a chest X-ray to diagnose tension pneumothorax. Immediate needle decompression (2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line, or 4th/5th intercostal space, anterior axillary line) is life-saving.
Q20
Which of the following would more appropriately be identified and treated as part of the secondary survey?
A. Pericardial tamponade B. Pelvic fracture with vaginal laceration C. Apnea from severe head injury D. Scalp laceration with BP 95/62
Answer: B — Pelvic fracture with vaginal laceration
Why? The primary survey focuses on immediately life-threatening conditions (airway, breathing, circulation). Pericardial tamponade and apnea are immediately life-threatening and are managed in the primary survey. A pelvic fracture with vaginal laceration is a significant injury but not immediately life-threatening unless it is causing massive hemorrhage. It is identified during the secondary survey (head-to-toe examination). A scalp laceration with borderline BP might require urgent attention, but it is not a primary survey priority like airway or breathing.
High-Yield: Secondary survey includes a detailed history (AMPLE: Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, Events of trauma) and a systematic head-to-toe examination, including pelvic and genital examinations.
Q21
A 26-year-old female comes to casualty following a high-impact blunt trauma. She is hypotensive with large-volume intraperitoneal fluid on ultrasound (FAST). She has received 2 units of whole blood but remains hypotensive. Which of the following is true regarding a damage control treatment strategy?
A. Damage control surgery aims to perform the definitive operation that patient requires as quickly as possible B. The patient should remain in casualty until she has been stabilized as this the best place for resuscitation C. Damage control resuscitation attempts to prevent or minimize the progression of the lethal triad and the systemic response to injury D. Damage control resuscitation requires that the patient be hemodynamically stable before surgery is undertaken E. As part of damage control resuscitation strategy, intentional cooling to hypothermic conditions allows for improved resuscitations
Answer: C — Damage control resuscitation attempts to prevent or minimize the progression of the lethal triad and the systemic response to injury
Why? Damage control resuscitation (DCR) is a strategy used in severely injured, exsanguinating patients to minimize physiological derangement. It aims to prevent or minimize the progression of the lethal triad (hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy) and systemic inflammatory response. This involves permissive hypotension (until hemorrhage is controlled), hemostatic resuscitation (balanced blood products, minimizing crystalloids), and rapid transfer to the operating room for damage control surgery (which is abbreviated, not definitive).
High-Yield: Damage control surgery involves: 1) Control of hemorrhage (packing, ligation), 2) Control of contamination (stapling bowel), 3) Temporary abdominal closure. The patient is then transferred to ICU for rewarming, correction of coagulopathy, and acidosis, followed by a planned re-laparotomy.
Q22
The "lethal triad" of trauma includes:
A. Hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy B. Coagulopathy, acidosis and shock C. Coagulopathy, shock and haemorrhage D. Acidosis, shock and haemorrhage
Answer: A — Hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy
Why? The "lethal triad" (or bloody vicious cycle) refers to the combination of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy in a severely bleeding trauma patient. Each component worsens the others: hypothermia impairs clotting enzyme function; acidosis (from poor perfusion) impairs clot formation; and coagulopathy leads to further bleeding, worsening shock and acidosis. It is a major cause of preventable death in trauma.
High-Yield: Prevention and treatment: warm the patient (fluids, blankets), stop the bleeding (surgery or angioembolization), and restore perfusion with blood products (balanced resuscitation). Avoid large volumes of cold crystalloids.
Q23
A 23-year-old man is brought to casualty following a motorcycle collision. His airway and breathing are noted to be intact, but he is hemorrhaging from his left lower extremity, which has suffered a traumatic amputation. While attempting to obtain intravenous access, he is noted to be hypotensive. Which of the following is true?
A. He will be coagulopathic due to dilution of clotting factors B. He will be hypercoagulable due to consumption of clotting factors C. He will be coagulopathic due to loss of clotting factors D. He will be hypercoagulable due to increased hepatic production of clotting factors E. He will be coagulopathic due to reduction in the hepatic production of clotting factors
Answer: C — He will be coagulopathic due to loss of clotting factors
Why? In a massive hemorrhage (such as a traumatic amputation), the patient loses whole blood, which includes red blood cells, plasma, and clotting factors. If resuscitation is delayed or only crystalloids are given, the remaining clotting factors are diluted further. The primary cause of coagulopathy in this acute setting is the physical loss of clotting factors through bleeding. (Note: Coagulopathy in trauma is multifactorial and also involves consumption, hypothermia, and acidosis, but option C is the most direct and correct statement provided).
High-Yield: Management of major hemorrhage: direct pressure, tourniquet (for limb amputation), and early activation of massive transfusion protocol (MTP) with a balanced ratio of PRBCs, FFP, and platelets (e.g., 1:1:1). Limit crystalloid use to avoid dilutional coagulopathy.
Q24
In a patient with blunt trauma who undergoes CT scan, all of the following are "soft signs" of blunt bowel and mesenteric injury EXCEPT:
A. Mesenteric haematoma B. Free air C. Mesenteric fat stranding D. Bowel wall thickening E. Free fluid in absence of solid organ injury
Answer: B — Free air
Why? On a CT scan for blunt abdominal trauma, "soft signs" of bowel/mesenteric injury include mesenteric haematoma, mesenteric fat stranding, bowel wall thickening, and unexplained free fluid. Free air (pneumoperitoneum) is a hard sign (definitive sign) of hollow viscus perforation, which requires emergent surgery. Soft signs may warrant observation, serial exams, or exploratory laparotomy depending on the clinical picture.
High-Yield: Signs of bowel perforation on CT: free intraperitoneal air, extravasation of oral contrast, and discontinuity of the bowel wall. Free air under the diaphragm is also seen on an erect CXR.
Q25
Recommendations for avoiding secondary brain injury include all of the following EXCEPT:
A. Maintain oxygen saturation >90% B. Maintain mild hypernatremia C. Maintain head of bed elevation of 30-45 degrees D. Maintain normotension E. Maintain mild hypercarbia
Answer: E — Maintain mild hypercarbia
Why? Secondary brain injury occurs from hypoxia, hypotension, hypercapnia, hypocapnia, hyperthermia, and hypo/hyperglycemia. To prevent secondary injury, one should maintain normocapnia (PaCO2 35–45 mmHg). Hypercarbia causes cerebral vasodilation, increasing intracranial pressure (ICP) and worsening cerebral edema. Hyperventilation (inducing hypocapnia) is only used temporarily as a bridge in cases of acute herniation, but mild hypercarbia is definitely harmful.
High-Yield: Avoid hypotension (systolic BP < 90 mmHg or MAP < 65 mmHg). Maintain head of bed at 30 degrees. Ensure adequate oxygenation (SpO2 > 90%). Avoid hyperthermia. Use osmotherapy (mannitol or hypertonic saline) for raised ICP.
Q26
Patients who undergo splenectomy should be vaccinated against which bacteria?
A. Streptococcus pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus B. Streptococcus pyogenes, Neisseria gonorrhea, Haemophilus influenza type B C. Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenza type B D. Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes and Neisseria meningitidis E. Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenza type B and Neisseria meningitidis
Answer: E — Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenza type B and Neisseria meningitidis
Why? Splenectomized patients are at high risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) from encapsulated bacteria. The three most important organisms to vaccinate against are: Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcal vaccine), Haemophilus influenzae type B (Hib vaccine), and Neisseria meningitidis (meningococcal vaccine). These should ideally be given at least 2 weeks before splenectomy, or 2 weeks after (if emergency).
High-Yield: Other post-splenectomy precautions: prophylactic penicillin (especially in children), patient education about the risk of infection and travel (malaria), and a medical alert card/bracelet. Vaccination against influenza and COVID-19 is also recommended.
Q27
Which of the following is true regarding comparison of ultrasound, DPL, and CT scan in evaluating abdominal trauma?
A. CT Scan is the best method of evaluating for abdominal injury in hypotensive patient B. CT is the exam for which the results are easiest to interpret C. Retroperitoneum is best assessed through DPL D. FAST can easily be integrated into the resuscitation process
Answer: D — FAST can easily be integrated into the resuscitation process
Why? FAST (Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma) is a bedside ultrasound examination that can be performed rapidly during the resuscitation of a trauma patient. It is non-invasive, repeatable, and does not require transport of the patient. This makes it easily integrated into the resuscitation process (e.g., during the primary survey). CT scan is contraindicated in unstable patients. DPL is invasive and less commonly used now.
High-Yield: FAST evaluates four windows: pericardial (for tamponade), perihepatic (Morison's pouch), perisplenic, and pelvic (for free fluid). It is highly sensitive for hemoperitoneum but does not identify specific organ injuries (CT is better for that).
Q28
Which is the least likely structure to be injured in pelvic trauma?
A. Vagina B. Bladder C. Rectum D. Ureter E. Presacral venous plexus
Answer: D — Ureter
Why? In pelvic trauma (e.g., pelvic fractures), structures located within the bony pelvis or closely associated with it are at risk. These include the bladder (especially if the pelvis fractures), urethra, rectum, vagina, and presacral venous plexus (which can cause massive retroperitoneal hemorrhage). The ureter runs retroperitoneally and is relatively protected; it is less commonly injured in blunt pelvic trauma compared to the bladder and urethra.
High-Yield: Pelvic fractures can be associated with massive hemorrhage from the presacral venous plexus or fracture sites. Open pelvic fractures (communication with vagina or rectum) have a very high mortality. A digital rectal and vaginal exam must be part of the trauma secondary survey.
Q29
Which of the following physical exam findings is most sensitive for the diagnosis of extremity compartment syndrome?
A. Swelling B. Absent distal pulses C. Pain on passive range of motion of the extremity D. Temperature difference between the affected limb (cold) and the perfused limb (warm) E. Presence of extremity paresthesia
Answer: C — Pain on passive range of motion of the extremity
Why? Compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency caused by increased pressure within a closed fascial compartment, leading to ischemia. The most sensitive and earliest sign is pain out of proportion to the injury and pain on passive stretch of the muscles in the affected compartment. Pulses are usually still present until late in the course, so absent distal pulses are a late and unreliable sign.
High-Yield: The "5 P's" of compartment syndrome: Pain (out of proportion, passive stretch), Pallor, Paresthesia, Paralysis, Pulselessness (late). Diagnosis: clinical + compartment pressure measurement (delta pressure < 30 mmHg indicates need for fasciotomy). Treatment: urgent fasciotomy.
Q30
An open fracture with a wound longer than 10 cm with extensive soft tissue damage that is able to be closed primarily is best classified as:
A. Gustilo Type IIIa B. Gustilo type I C. Gustilo type II D. Gustilo type IIIB
Answer: A — Gustilo Type IIIa
Why? The Gustilo-Anderson classification of open fractures:
Type I: Wound < 1 cm, clean.
Type II: Wound 1–10 cm, without extensive soft tissue damage.
Type III: Wound > 10 cm, high-energy injury, extensive soft tissue damage.
Type IIIa: Adequate soft tissue coverage despite extensive damage (can be closed primarily).
Type IIIb: Inadequate soft tissue coverage requiring flap reconstruction.
Type IIIc: Associated with arterial injury requiring repair.
The scenario describes a wound >10 cm with extensive damage but able to be closed primarily, which fits Type IIIa.
High-Yield: Open fractures require urgent irrigation and debridement (I&D), tetanus prophylaxis, and antibiotics (e.g., cefazolin, plus gentamicin for Type III). IIIb/IIIc carry a high risk of amputation and require complex reconstruction.
Clinical Scenario 1
A 36-year-old man with a history of abdominal tuberculosis presents to the emergency with 2 days history of increasing abdominal pain. The pain is diffuse, although most intense in the peri-umbilical region. On questioning, he notes that he has not had a bowel movement since the onset of the pain, nor has he vomited. On initial physical examination, his abdomen is very distended and tender to palpation. One hour later, his clinical condition has worsened. Which of the following additional finding would most indicate the need for emergent surgery?
A. Absence of bowel sounds B. Absence of flatus C. Bilious vomiting D. Diffuse rebound tenderness
Answer: D — Diffuse rebound tenderness
Why? The patient likely has a small bowel obstruction (SBO) secondary to adhesions from abdominal TB. A key indicator for emergent surgery in SBO is the presence of peritonitis (e.g., diffuse rebound tenderness, guarding, rigidity) or signs of strangulation (fever, tachycardia, severe pain). Diffuse rebound tenderness suggests bowel ischemia/perforation, requiring immediate laparotomy. Absence of bowel sounds and flatus are expected in obstruction but do not alone dictate emergent surgery.
High-Yield: Absolute indications for surgery in SBO: peritonitis, strangulation (fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis), closed-loop obstruction, and failure of conservative management (NG tube + IV fluids) after 24-48 hours. TB strictures are a common cause of SBO in endemic areas.
Clinical Scenario 2
A 42-year-old lady with a prior history of intermittent right upper quadrant (RUQ) abdominal pain following meals, presents with a more severe episode. She has nausea, vomiting, and fever (38.2°C). She is obese and has RUQ tenderness. Ultrasound reveals gallstones. Labs: WBC 13,000 (elevated), Total bilirubin 3.8 mg/dL (elevated), AST 130, ALT 150, ALP 300 (all elevated), Amylase <30 (normal), Lipase 8 (normal). Which of the following is most likely responsible for her symptoms and laboratory values?
A. A stone obstructing her common bile duct B. A stone obstructing her cystic duct C. A stone obstructing her pancreatic duct/ampulla of Vater D. Stones in her gallbladder
Answer: A — A stone obstructing her common bile duct
Why? The patient has signs of choledocholithiasis (a stone in the common bile duct) causing biliary obstruction. This is evidenced by the elevated bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and transaminases, along with fever and RUQ pain (Charcot's triad suggests cholangitis). A stone in the cystic duct (B) would cause biliary colic or cholecystitis but typically would not cause significant jaundice or elevated ALP. Pancreatic duct obstruction (C) would cause elevated amylase/lipase (pancreatitis), which are normal here. Gallstones in the gallbladder (D) alone do not cause obstruction unless they migrate.
High-Yield: Choledocholithiasis is best diagnosed with MRCP or ERCP. If cholangitis is present, urgent ERCP is required for biliary drainage and stone extraction, along with IV antibiotics.
Clinical Scenario 3
An obese 46-year-old, multiparous woman presents to the physician with nonradiating right upper quadrant pain and fever that was preceded by nausea and vomiting. Ultrasonography shows hyperechogenic structures in the right upper quadrant. Laboratory testing reveals a WBC count of 14,500/mm3, an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 40 mm/hr, and a serum amylase level of 70 U/L. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis in this patient?
A. Acute acalculous cholecystitis B. Acute calculous cholecystitis C. Acute pancreatitis D. Carcinoma of the pancreas
Answer: B — Acute calculous cholecystitis
Why? The patient has RUQ pain, fever, nausea, and vomiting. Ultrasound shows hyperechogenic structures in the RUQ, which are gallstones (with acoustic shadowing). A normal amylase rules out acute pancreatitis. Acalculous cholecystitis occurs in critically ill patients without gallstones. The combination of gallstones on ultrasound and clinical signs of inflammation (fever, leukocytosis) points to acute calculous cholecystitis.
High-Yield: The "5 F's" of gallstone risk: Female, Fat, Forty, Fertile, and Family history. Murphy's sign (inspiratory arrest on RUQ palpation) is highly specific for acute cholecystitis. Treatment: IV antibiotics, analgesia, and early laparoscopic cholecystectomy.
Clinical Scenario 4
A 34-year-old man develops an enterocutaneous fistula originating in the ileum secondary to abdominal tuberculosis. Which of the following would be the most appropriate fluid for replacement of his enteric losses?
A. D5W B. 3% normal saline C. Ringer's lactate solution D. 0.9% sodium chloride
Answer: C — Ringer's lactate solution
Why? Ileal fluid losses are rich in sodium, potassium, chloride, and bicarbonate. Ringer's lactate is the most physiological crystalloid for replacement because it contains sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, and lactate (which is metabolized to bicarbonate, helping correct the metabolic acidosis caused by bicarbonate loss). Normal saline (0.9%) can cause hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis if given in large volumes. D5W provides no electrolytes.
High-Yield: High-output fistulas (>500 mL/day) require careful fluid and electrolyte replacement, parenteral nutrition, and protection of the surrounding skin (stoma appliance or barrier cream). Somatostatin analogues (octreotide) can reduce output.
Clinical Scenario 5
A 45-year-old postmenopausal lady presents with a hard lump measuring 6x4 cm in the left breast, which is fixed to the pectoralis major muscle but free from the skin. She also has fixed axillary nodes on the same side and no clinical evidence of metastasis. What is the TNM stage of the lady's tumour?
A. T4N1M0 B. T4N2M0 C. T3N1M0 D. T3N2M0
Answer: B — T4N2M0 (Note: If the tumour is fixed to the pectoralis muscle, it is T4 (invasion of chest wall). If it is fixed to the skin, it is also T4. Fixed axillary nodes are N2. No metastasis is M0. So T4N2M0 is correct. However, if the tumour is 6x4 cm (T3) and fixed to muscle is T4, the highest T stage applies. Fixed axillary nodes = N2. Therefore, T4N2M0.)
Why? Breast cancer TNM staging:
T: T1 ≤2 cm, T2 >2–5 cm, T3 >5 cm, T4 = any size with direct extension to chest wall (fixed to pectoralis) and/or skin.
N: N0 = no nodes, N1 = movable ipsilateral axillary nodes, N2 = fixed/matted ipsilateral axillary nodes.
M: M0 = no distant metastasis.
Therefore, a 6 cm tumour fixed to the pectoralis muscle is T4, and fixed axillary nodes are N2. The stage is T4N2M0 (Stage IIIB).
High-Yield: Inflammatory breast cancer is always T4d. Fixation to the chest wall (pectoralis) or skin (ulceration, satellite nodules) is T4. Staging guides treatment: neoadjuvant chemotherapy is often used for locally advanced (Stage III) disease before surgery.
Clinical Scenario 6
A 70-kg man with pyloric obstruction secondary to ulcer disease is admitted to the hospital for resuscitation after 1 week of prolonged vomiting. What metabolic disturbance is expected?
A. Hypokalemic, hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis B. Hyperkalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis C. Hyperkalemic, hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis D. Hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis
Answer: D — Hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis
Why? Prolonged vomiting from pyloric obstruction leads to the loss of gastric acid (HCl), causing a loss of hydrogen and chloride ions. This results in a hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis. Additionally, the loss of potassium in the vomit and the kidneys' compensatory response (excreting H+ and retaining Na+ at the expense of K+) leads to hypokalemia. This classic triad is known as hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis.
High-Yield: Treatment: IV fluid resuscitation with normal saline (to replace chloride) and potassium replacement. A nasogastric tube may be needed to decompress the stomach. Once the patient is stabilized, definitive surgery for the ulcer disease is considered.
Clinical Scenario 8
A 35-year-old man presents to the surgical clinic with a suspected direct inguinal hernia. These will pass through Hesselbach's triangle. Which of the following forms the medial edge of this structure?
A. External oblique aponeurosis B. Inferior epigastric artery C. Inferior epigastric vein D. Rectus abdominis E. Iliohypogastric nerve
Answer: D — Rectus abdominis
Why? Hesselbach's triangle (the site of direct inguinal hernias) is bounded by:
Medial: Rectus abdominis muscle.
Lateral: Inferior epigastric artery.
Inferior: Inguinal ligament.
Therefore, the medial border is the rectus abdominis.
High-Yield: Direct hernias pass through Hesselbach's triangle (medial to inferior epigastric vessels). Indirect hernias pass through the deep inguinal ring (lateral to inferior epigastric vessels). Femoral hernias pass below the inguinal ligament.
Clinical Scenario 9
A 53-year-old man presents with an inguinal hernia. Which of the following surface landmarks may be used to identify the location of the deep inguinal ring?
A. 4 cm superior to the femoral vein B. 4 cm inferomedially to the femoral artery C. Mid inguinal point D. Halfway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the symphysis pubis E. Mid point of the inguinal ligament
Answer: C — Mid inguinal point
Why? The deep inguinal ring is located approximately 1.25 cm above the midpoint of the inguinal ligament. The mid inguinal point (midway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the pubic symphysis) is the surface landmark used to identify the deep ring. The midpoint of the inguinal ligament is between the ASIS and the pubic tubercle; the mid inguinal point is slightly different.
High-Yield: The deep inguinal ring is the entrance to the inguinal canal. In indirect hernias, the herniated bowel enters through the deep ring. In direct hernias, it protrudes through the posterior wall of the canal (Hesselbach's triangle).
Clinical Scenario 10
Which of the following statements regarding Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) are true?
A. The maximum score is 15 B. The minimum score is 0 C. A GCS of 10 means the patient is in coma D. Eye opening to command/speech is scored as 2 E. A GCS of 13 means the patient is in coma
Answer: A — The maximum score is 15
Why? The GCS maximum is 15 (E4 + V5 + M6). The minimum is 3 (E1 + V1 + M1), not 0. A GCS of 8 or less is generally considered coma (unable to protect airway). Eye opening to speech is 3, not 2. A GCS of 13 is mild impairment, not coma.
High-Yield: GCS is used to assess level of consciousness. It is divided into three categories: eye opening (E), verbal response (V), and motor response (M). A GCS of 8 or less is a severe head injury and requires intubation for airway protection.
Clinical Scenario 11
When does primary brain injury occur?
A. At the moment of impact B. In the first hour after injury C. In the first 4 hours after injury D. Only if the patient is in coma E. In the first 24 hours after injury
Answer: A — At the moment of impact
Why? Primary brain injury occurs at the time of the traumatic impact. It is the direct result of mechanical forces (e.g., acceleration/deceleration, impact) causing tissue damage, such as contusions, lacerations, diffuse axonal injury, or hematomas. It cannot be prevented or reversed. The goal of medical management is to prevent secondary brain injury.
High-Yield: Secondary brain injury occurs minutes to days after the initial trauma due to hypoxia, hypotension, hyperthermia, hypercarbia, and intracranial hypertension. It is preventable and treatable with proper resuscitation and monitoring.
Clinical Scenario 12
Which of the following can cause secondary brain injury?
A. Hypoxia B. Hypotension C. Raised ICP D. Reduced cerebral perfusion E. All the above
Answer: E — All the above
Why? Secondary brain injury is caused by a cascade of systemic and intracranial events that occur after the primary injury. All the listed factors (hypoxia, hypotension, raised intracranial pressure (ICP), and reduced cerebral perfusion) can cause or exacerbate secondary brain injury. They lead to ischemia, cell death, and worsening neurological outcome.
High-Yield: Management of severe TBI focuses on preventing these secondary insults: maintain SpO2 > 90%, systolic BP > 90 mmHg, normal PaCO2 (35-45 mmHg), head of bed at 30°, and treat raised ICP with osmotherapy (mannitol/hypertonic saline) and sedation.
Clinical Scenario 13
Which of the following are causes of adynamic intestinal obstruction?
A. Paralytic ileus B. Hypokalemia C. Mechanical obstruction D. Volvulus
Answer: A and B — Paralytic ileus and Hypokalemia
Why? Adynamic (functional) intestinal obstruction is due to the cessation of bowel motility without a physical obstruction. Causes include postoperative ileus, electrolyte imbalances (especially hypokalemia), medications (opioids), sepsis, and retroperitoneal hematoma. Mechanical obstruction (volvulus, adhesions, tumors) involves a physical barrier. Therefore, paralytic ileus and hypokalemia are causes of adynamic obstruction.
High-Yield: Paralytic ileus presents with a distended, silent abdomen, no flatus, and nausea/vomiting. Management: bowel rest, NG decompression, IV fluid resuscitation, and correction of electrolytes. Prokinetic agents are not routinely recommended.
Clinical Scenario 14
Which of the following does not contribute to dehydration and electrolyte loss in intestinal obstruction?
A. Reduced oral intake B. Defective intestinal absorption C. Vomiting D. Diarrhoea E. Sequestration in the bowel lumen
Answer: D — Diarrhoea
Why? In intestinal obstruction, the bowel becomes blocked, preventing the normal passage of intestinal contents. This leads to dehydration and electrolyte loss primarily through: vomiting, reduced oral intake, defective absorption by the bowel, and sequestration of fluids in the bowel lumen. Diarrhoea is not a feature of intestinal obstruction; in fact, obstipation (inability to pass stool or gas) is a hallmark. Diarrhoea can occur in partial obstruction or as overflow diarrhoea, but it is not a primary mechanism of fluid loss in a complete obstruction.
High-Yield: Fluid sequestration in the obstructed bowel can be massive (up to several liters), leading to third-space losses and hypovolemia. This is why aggressive IV fluid resuscitation is critical in the management of SBO.
Clinical Scenario 15
Which of the following statements regarding strangulation in intestinal obstruction are true?
A. The arterial supply is compromised before the venous return B. Marked translocation and systemic exposure to anaerobic organisms occur C. The morbidity of strangulation due to an external hernia is greater than intraperitoneal strangulation D. The morbidity and mortality in strangulation are independent of patient age
Answer: B — Marked translocation and systemic exposure to anaerobic organisms occur
Why? Strangulation occurs when the blood supply to the obstructed bowel is compromised. Initially, the venous return is obstructed, followed by arterial compromise. This leads to ischemia, necrosis, and perforation. As the bowel becomes ischemic, the mucosal barrier breaks down, allowing translocation of bacteria (especially anaerobes) and toxins into the systemic circulation, causing sepsis and shock.
High-Yield: Strangulation is a surgical emergency. Signs include severe, constant pain (not colicky), fever, tachycardia, peritonitis, and leukocytosis. Treatment: urgent laparotomy with resection of necrotic bowel.
