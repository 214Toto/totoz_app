Question 1 – Concerning the Cell
a) Using a diagram ONLY, describe the cell membrane according to the fluid mosaic model of Singer and Nicholson (5 marks)
Diagram should show:
• A phospholipid bilayer with hydrophilic heads facing outward (ECF and ICF) and hydrophobic tails facing inward.
• Integral proteins embedded within the bilayer (some spanning the entire membrane = transmembrane proteins).
• Peripheral proteins attached to the inner or outer surface.
• Glycoproteins and glycolipids on the extracellular surface (cell recognition).
• Cholesterol molecules interspersed within the hydrophobic core.
• Label: extracellular fluid, intracellular fluid, phospholipid bilayer, integral protein, peripheral protein, glycoprotein, cholesterol, channel protein.
b) List five functions of membrane proteins (5 marks)
1. Transport: Channel and carrier proteins move ions and molecules across the membrane (e.g., Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, glucose transporters).
2. Receptors: Bind hormones, neurotransmitters, and other ligands, initiating signal transduction (e.g., insulin receptor, β-adrenergic receptor).
3. Enzymes: Catalyse chemical reactions at the membrane surface (e.g., adenylyl cyclase, ACE).
4. Cell adhesion: Anchor cells to each other or to the extracellular matrix (e.g., cadherins, integrins).
5. Cell recognition: Glycoproteins act as identity markers for immune recognition and cell-cell interactions (e.g., MHC proteins).
Also: Structural support, signal transduction, and acting as receptors for signalling molecules.
c) Mention five characteristics of facilitated transport (5 marks)
1. Requires a carrier protein (transporter) in the membrane — it is not simple diffusion.
2. Passive process: Does not require ATP (energy-independent).
3. Down the concentration gradient: Moves solute from high to low concentration (downhill).
4. Specificity: The carrier is specific for a particular solute (e.g., GLUT4 for glucose).
5. Saturable: There is a maximum transport rate (Tm) when all carriers are occupied (shows Michaelis-Menten kinetics).
6. Can be inhibited by competitive inhibitors.
d) List five functions of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum (5 marks)
1. Lipid synthesis: Synthesises phospholipids and cholesterol for membrane formation.
2. Steroid hormone synthesis: Produces steroid hormones in endocrine cells (e.g., cortisol, testosterone).
3. Detoxification: Detoxifies drugs and metabolic waste products (especially in liver cells).
4. Calcium storage: Stores and releases Ca²⁺ (in muscle cells it is called the sarcoplasmic reticulum).
5. Carbohydrate metabolism: Involved in glycogen metabolism and glucose-6-phosphatase activity in liver.
6. Lipoprotein synthesis: Produces lipoproteins for transport of lipids in the blood.
Question 2 – Concerning Excitable Tissues
a) State the "All or None law" and state three structures that obey this law (5 marks)
All-or-None Law: When a stimulus reaches threshold intensity, an action potential is generated with a constant amplitude and duration, regardless of further increases in stimulus strength. If the stimulus is below threshold, no action potential is produced. The response is therefore "all or none."

Structures that obey this law:
1. Single nerve fibre (axon) — a threshold stimulus produces a full action potential.
2. Skeletal muscle fibre — once threshold is reached, the muscle fibre contracts maximally.
3. Cardiac muscle fibre — each fibre obeys the all-or-none law (though the heart as a whole can be graded by recruitment).
Note: The law applies to single excitable cells, not to whole nerves or muscles (which are composed of many units with different thresholds).
b) Define a chronaxie and mention its importances (4 marks)
Chronaxie: The minimum time required to stimulate a nerve or muscle with a current of twice the rheobase (the minimum current that can excite the tissue given infinite time). It is a measure of the excitability of the tissue — the shorter the chronaxie, the more excitable the tissue.

Importances:
1. Assessment of excitability: Chronaxie is used clinically to assess the excitability of nerves and muscles (e.g., in denervation injuries, the chronaxie is prolonged).
2. Diagnosis of nerve lesions: Helps differentiate between complete and partial denervation — denervated muscle has a much longer chronaxie.
3. Electrotherapy: Used to select appropriate pulse durations for electrical stimulation of nerves and muscles.
4. Comparative physiology: Allows comparison of excitability between different tissues and species.
c) The resting membrane potential (RMP) of the nerve is -70mV. State and explain two factors responsible for producing the RMP (6 marks)
1. Selective permeability of the membrane to K⁺ (K⁺ leak channels):
The resting membrane is highly permeable to K⁺ and only slightly permeable to Na⁺. K⁺ ions diffuse out of the cell down their concentration gradient through leak channels, leaving behind negatively charged proteins and other anions. This outward movement of K⁺ (efflux) creates a negative charge inside the cell. The membrane potential approaches the K⁺ equilibrium potential (approximately -90 mV), but is slightly less negative due to a small Na⁺ leak.

2. Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump:
The electrogenic Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase actively transports 3 Na⁺ ions out of the cell for every 2 K⁺ ions pumped in, using ATP. This contributes a small negative charge to the inside of the cell (about -4 mV) and maintains the concentration gradients of Na⁺ and K⁺ necessary for the resting potential. Without this pump, the ionic gradients would eventually run down and the RMP would be lost.

Summary: The RMP is primarily generated by the K⁺ diffusion potential and maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump.
d) Briefly describe the mechanism of neuromuscular transmission (5 marks)
1. Action potential arrival: An action potential travels down the motor neuron axon to the presynaptic terminal (bouton).
2. Ca²⁺ influx: Depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels in the presynaptic membrane. Ca²⁺ enters the terminal.
3. Neurotransmitter release: Ca²⁺ triggers the fusion of synaptic vesicles with the presynaptic membrane, releasing acetylcholine (ACh) into the synaptic cleft via exocytosis.
4. Receptor binding: ACh diffuses across the cleft and binds to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChR) on the postsynaptic muscle membrane (motor end plate).
5. Depolarization and action potential: Binding of ACh opens ligand-gated Na⁺ channels, causing Na⁺ influx and depolarization of the muscle membrane. If threshold is reached, an action potential is generated in the muscle fibre, leading to contraction.
6. Termination: Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) in the synaptic cleft rapidly hydrolyses ACh into acetate and choline, terminating the signal.
e) Briefly explain the mechanism of skeletal muscle relaxation (5 marks)
1. Cessation of action potentials: When motor neuron stimulation stops, action potentials cease, and no further ACh is released.
2. Ca²⁺ reuptake: The sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) actively pumps Ca²⁺ back into its lumen using Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA pump). This requires ATP.
3. Ca²⁺ dissociation: As cytosolic Ca²⁺ concentration falls, Ca²⁺ dissociates from troponin C.
4. Tropomyosin returns: Without Ca²⁺ bound to troponin, tropomyosin returns to its resting position, covering the myosin-binding sites on actin.
5. Cross-bridge detachment: Myosin heads detach from actin (ATP is required for this step). The muscle fibre returns to its resting length passively (due to elastic recoil of connective tissue and antagonistic muscle action).
Key point: Relaxation is an active process requiring ATP for Ca²⁺ reuptake and cross-bridge detachment.
Question 3 – Concerning Blood and Body Fluids
a) Briefly describe the composition of extracellular fluid (5 marks)
Extracellular fluid (ECF) constitutes about 20% of total body weight (approximately 14 L in a 70 kg adult). It is divided into:

1. Interstitial fluid: The fluid surrounding cells, constituting about 15% of body weight (approximately 11 L). It provides the immediate environment for cells and is the medium for exchange of nutrients and waste products.
2. Plasma: The fluid portion of blood, constituting about 5% of body weight (approximately 3 L). It contains dissolved proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, and waste products.
3. Transcellular fluid: A specialised compartment (about 1–2 L) including cerebrospinal fluid, synovial fluid, aqueous humour, pleural, pericardial, and peritoneal fluids.

Composition: ECF is rich in Na⁺, Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻, and nutrients (glucose, amino acids), with low K⁺, Mg²⁺, and proteins compared to ICF. It is the internal environment of the body (milieu intérieur).
b) Give three reasons why infants and children are more vulnerable to develop dehydration than adults (3 marks)
1. Higher total body water (TBW): Infants have a higher percentage of body water (about 70–80% of body weight) compared to adults (about 60%), with a larger proportion in the ECF. This means greater water loss for a given degree of dehydration.
2. Higher surface area to volume ratio: Infants have a larger body surface area relative to their volume, leading to greater insensible water loss through the skin and lungs.
3. Immature kidneys: The kidneys of infants are less developed and have a reduced ability to concentrate urine (lower renal concentrating capacity), meaning they excrete more water to eliminate solutes, predisposing them to dehydration.
Also: Higher metabolic rate (increased heat production and evaporative loss), inability to independently access water, and higher respiratory rate.
c) Mention five manifestations of dehydration (5 marks)
1. Dry lips, mouth, and tongue (decreased saliva production).
2. Dry skin with reduced turgor (skin tents when pinched).
3. Concentrated urine (dark yellow, low volume) and decreased urine output (oliguria).
4. Headache, dizziness, and light-headedness (due to reduced blood volume and cerebral perfusion).
5. Constipation (due to decreased water in the colon).
6. Tachycardia and hypotension (compensatory mechanisms for hypovolaemia).
7. Sunken eyes and fontanelle in infants.
8. Lethargy and confusion in severe cases.
d) What is hematocrit? Mention three physiological factors that affect hematocrit (5 marks)
Hematocrit (packed cell volume, PCV): The percentage of blood volume occupied by red blood cells after centrifugation. Normal values: men 42–52%, women 36–46%.

Physiological factors affecting hematocrit:
1. Age: Higher in newborns (55–65%), decreases in childhood, and slightly decreases in the elderly.
2. Sex: Higher in males than females due to testosterone stimulating erythropoiesis and the absence of menstrual blood loss in males.
3. Altitude: Chronic hypoxia at high altitude stimulates erythropoietin production, increasing RBC count and hematocrit.
4. Hydration status: Dehydration increases hematocrit (haemoconcentration); overhydration decreases it (haemodilution).
5. Pregnancy: Physiological anaemia of pregnancy due to increased plasma volume > RBC mass, lowering hematocrit.
6. Physical activity: Intense exercise can transiently increase hematocrit due to hemoconcentration.
e) Explain the fibrinolytic system (10 marks)
The fibrinolytic system is responsible for the dissolution of blood clots (fibrinolysis) after haemostasis has been achieved and tissue repair has occurred. It prevents excessive clotting and maintains blood fluidity.

Components and mechanism:
1. Plasminogen: An inactive zymogen produced by the liver, circulating in plasma. It is incorporated into the clot during formation.
2. Plasminogen activators:
   - Tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA): Released by endothelial cells in response to injury, stasis, or thrombin. It binds to fibrin and efficiently converts plasminogen to plasmin.
   - Urokinase: Produced by the kidneys and found in urine; also activates plasminogen.
   - Streptokinase: A bacterial product used therapeutically.
3. Plasmin formation: Plasminogen activators cleave plasminogen to form plasmin, a proteolytic enzyme.
4. Fibrin degradation: Plasmin digests fibrin and fibrinogen into soluble fibrin degradation products (FDPs), including D-dimers. This dissolves the clot.
5. Inhibition: The system is tightly regulated by:
   - Plasminogen activator inhibitors (PAI-1, PAI-2): Inhibit t-PA and urokinase.
   - α₂-antiplasmin: Rapidly inactivates free plasmin in plasma.
   - Thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI): Removes lysine residues from fibrin, reducing plasminogen binding.

Clinical significance: t-PA is used as a thrombolytic agent in myocardial infarction and stroke. D-dimer is a marker of fibrinolysis and is used in the diagnosis of DVT and pulmonary embolism. Impaired fibrinolysis contributes to thrombosis; excessive fibrinolysis causes bleeding.
f) List the twelve clotting factors in order (6 marks)
The clotting factors are numbered I to XIII (with factor VI being obsolete):

I. Fibrinogen
II. Prothrombin
III. Thromboplastin (tissue factor)
IV. Calcium (Ca²⁺)
V. Labile factor (proaccelerin)
VI. Obsolete (not used)
VII. Stable factor (proconvertin)
VIII. Antihemophilic factor (AHF)
IX. Christmas factor (plasma thromboplastin component, PTC)
X. Stuart-Prower factor
XI. Plasma thromboplastin antecedent (PTA)
XII. Hageman factor
XIII. Fibrin stabilizing factor (fibrinase)
