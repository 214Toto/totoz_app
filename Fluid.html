1 Homeostasis

Define Homeostasis. Draw or describe the basic physiological feedback balance loop showing how external changes or internal malfunctions impact health.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Homeostasis: The maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment within the body despite changes in the external environment. It involves dynamic equilibrium of body fluids, electrolytes, temperature, pH, etc.

Feedback loop: A sensor detects a change (stimulus) → sends signal to a control center (e.g., hypothalamus) → control center activates an effector (e.g., kidney, sweat glands) → effector produces a response that counteracts the initial change (negative feedback). External changes or internal malfunctions (e.g., disease) can disrupt this loop, leading to homeostatic imbalance and pathology.

2 TBW Distribution

Outline the distribution of Total Body Water (TBW) in a standard 70 kg male, specifying the absolute volumes and proportions of the Intracellular Fluid (ICF), Extracellular Fluid (ECF), Interstitial Fluid, and Plasma compartments.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

In a 70 kg adult male, TBW is approximately 60% of body weight = 42 L.

  • Intracellular Fluid (ICF): 28 L (40% of body weight, 2/3 of TBW)
  • Extracellular Fluid (ECF): 14 L (20% of body weight, 1/3 of TBW)
  • Interstitial Fluid: ~11 L (3/4 of ECF)
  • Plasma: ~3 L (1/4 of ECF)

Transcellular fluid (~1 L) is a small subset of ECF.

4 Electrolyte Composition

Compare the electrolyte composition of Plasma, Interstitial Fluid, and Intracellular Fluid, highlighting the key cations and anions predominant in each.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

  • Plasma: Major cation Na⁺ (142 mEq/L); major anions Cl⁻ (103) and HCO₃⁻ (24). Contains plasma proteins (e.g., albumin).
  • Interstitial Fluid: Similar to plasma but much lower protein content. Major cation Na⁺ (145); major anions Cl⁻ (114) and HCO₃⁻ (30).
  • Intracellular Fluid: Major cation K⁺ (150); major anions phosphate (PO₄³⁻) and negatively charged proteins. Low Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

5 Indicator Dilution Technique

State the fundamental mathematical formula for the Indicator (Dye) Dilution Technique. List four essential criteria required for a substance to be considered a suitable indicator dye.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Formula: Volume = (Amount of indicator injected) / (Concentration of indicator after equilibrium)

Criteria for a suitable indicator:

  • Non-toxic and harmless to the body.
  • Distributes evenly and rapidly throughout the compartment being measured.
  • Does not leak out of the compartment or get metabolized/excreted during the test period.
  • Can be accurately measured in low concentrations (high sensitivity).

6 Total Blood Volume

Explain how Total Blood Volume is calculated using Plasma Volume and Hematocrit values.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Total Blood Volume = Plasma Volume / (1 − Hematocrit)

Where Hematocrit is the fraction of blood volume occupied by red blood cells (e.g., 0.45). For example, if Plasma Volume = 3 L and Hematocrit = 0.45, then Total Blood Volume = 3 / (1 − 0.45) = 5.45 L.

7 Osmolarity vs Osmolality

Differentiate clearly between Osmolarity and Osmolality. State why Osmolality is more accurate and clinically preferred.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Osmolarity: Concentration of osmotically active particles per liter of solution (mOsm/L). It depends on solution volume, which can change with temperature and pressure.

Osmolality: Concentration of osmotically active particles per kilogram of solvent (mOsm/kg). It depends on the mass of solvent, which is not affected by temperature or pressure.

Clinical preference: Osmolality is more accurate because the mass of solvent (water) remains constant regardless of temperature and pressure, whereas volume can change. Therefore, osmolality is preferred in clinical settings.

8 Tonicity

Define Tonicity. Differentiate between Isotonic, Hypotonic, and Hypertonic solutions, giving ONE clinical example of a solution for each.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Tonicity: The ability of an extracellular solution to cause water to move into or out of a cell, thereby affecting cell volume.

  • Isotonic: Same solute concentration as intracellular fluid; no net water movement; cell volume unchanged. Example: 0.9% NaCl (normal saline).
  • Hypotonic: Lower solute concentration than intracellular fluid; water moves into the cell; cell swells. Example: 0.45% NaCl (half-normal saline).
  • Hypertonic: Higher solute concentration than intracellular fluid; water moves out of the cell; cell shrinks. Example: 3% NaCl (hypertonic saline).

9 Gibbs-Donnan Effect

Describe the mechanism of the Gibbs-Donnan Effect across cell membranes and explain its effect on ion distribution and water movement.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Mechanism: The Gibbs-Donnan Effect arises because of the presence of non-diffusible charged proteins inside the cell. These negatively charged proteins attract cations (e.g., K⁺, Na⁺) and repel anions (e.g., Cl⁻), leading to an unequal distribution of diffusible ions across the membrane. This creates a Donnan equilibrium with a membrane potential and an osmotic gradient.

Effect on ion distribution: There is a higher concentration of diffusible cations (Na⁺) on the inside and higher diffusible anions (Cl⁻) on the outside than would be predicted by simple diffusion. Effect on water: The increased osmolarity inside the cell (due to more particles, including the proteins) causes water to move into the cell, tending to swell and potentially burst it.

10 Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase Pump

Explain how the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase pump restores physiological cell volume homeostasis against the osmotic threat posed by the Gibbs-Donnan Effect.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

The Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase pump actively extrudes three Na⁺ ions out of the cell and pumps two K⁺ ions into the cell for each ATP consumed. This creates a net outward movement of ions, which is osmotically equivalent to removing one osmotically active particle per cycle.

This active transport counteracts the osmotic gradient created by the Donnan effect: by pumping Na⁺ out, it reduces the intracellular osmolarity, thereby reducing the tendency for water to enter the cell. It maintains the low intracellular Na⁺ concentration, balancing the osmotic pressure and keeping the cell volume stable. If the pump fails (e.g., due to ATP depletion), the cell swells and may burst (lyse).

1 Dehydration & ECF Loss

A patient presents to the emergency room with severe dehydration following 3 days of intense vomiting and diarrhea. Explain how severe loss of Extracellular Fluid (ECF) alters long-term blood pressure control and serum ECF osmolarity.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

ECF loss → decreased blood volume → decreased venous return → decreased cardiac output → decreased blood pressure. Long-term blood pressure control: The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) is activated. Reduced renal perfusion leads to renin release → angiotensin II → aldosterone → Na⁺ and water retention by kidneys, and vasoconstriction. ADH (vasopressin) is also released due to increased plasma osmolarity, promoting water reabsorption. If fluid loss continues, these compensatory mechanisms may fail, leading to hypovolemic shock.

Serum ECF osmolarity: Loss of hypotonic fluid (vomiting/diarrhea) initially may cause hypernatremia and increased osmolarity if water loss exceeds sodium loss. The high osmolarity stimulates thirst and ADH release, but if water intake is insufficient, hyperosmolar dehydration ensues, causing cellular dehydration.

2 Indicator Dilution Calculation

A clinical laboratory researcher wants to measure the fluid compartment volumes of an experimental subject. They inject 1 mL of a 10 mg/mL indicator solution into a compartment. After equilibrium, the final indicator concentration is measured as 0.01 mg/mL. Calculate the volume of this fluid compartment showing all steps.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Given: Volume injected = 1 mL; Initial concentration = 10 mg/mL; Final concentration = 0.01 mg/mL.

Amount of indicator injected = Volume × Concentration = 1 mL × 10 mg/mL = 10 mg.

Formula: Volume of compartment = Amount / Final concentration = 10 mg / 0.01 mg/mL = 1000 mL (1 L).

Therefore, the volume of the fluid compartment is 1000 mL.

3 Protein Malnutrition & Fluid Balance

In severe protein malnutrition (such as Kwashiorkor or severe starvation), plasma protein levels (e.g., albumin) drop drastically. Using your knowledge of body fluid distribution and plasma composition, explain how reduced plasma protein concentration disrupts fluid balance between plasma and interstitial spaces.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Plasma proteins (mainly albumin) are responsible for the colloid osmotic pressure (oncotic pressure) of plasma, which draws water from the interstitial space into the capillaries. When plasma protein levels drop, colloid osmotic pressure decreases. Consequently, the balance of Starling forces shifts: the reduced oncotic pressure cannot oppose the hydrostatic pressure effectively, so fluid moves out of the capillaries into the interstitial space. This leads to interstitial edema (swelling), which is characteristic of Kwashiorkor. Additionally, reduced plasma volume can trigger compensatory mechanisms (RAAS, ADH) that further worsen fluid retention and edema.

4 Marathon Runner & Water Balance

An elite marathon runner completes a 42 km race in intense heat. According to daily water balance data, their sweat loss reaches 5000 mL/day and insensible lung loss increases to 650 mL/day. Compare their daily intake and output requirements to a normal resting individual, and predict what happens to urinary output.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Normal resting individual: Daily water intake ~2500 mL (food + drink); output: urine ~1500 mL, insensible loss (skin + lungs) ~900 mL, sweat ~100 mL, feces ~100 mL.

Marathon runner: Sweat loss = 5000 mL, insensible lung loss = 650 mL (plus skin ~350 mL) → total insensible + sweat loss ~6000 mL. To maintain water balance, intake must increase to about 6000 mL/day. Urinary output will be significantly reduced (to less than 500 mL/day) due to compensatory ADH release, as the body tries to conserve water. Without adequate fluid intake, the runner will become dehydrated, leading to hypernatremia, decreased blood volume, and potentially heat stroke.

5 Ischemic Hypoxia & Donnan Effect

A patient is admitted with ischemic cellular hypoxia where cellular ATP production ceases completely. Explain what will happen to the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase pump activity, the internal concentration of Na⁺ and Cl⁻, and the structural physical integrity of the cells as a result of the Donnan Effect.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase pump activity: Without ATP, the pump ceases to function. Normally it extrudes 3 Na⁺ and imports 2 K⁺. Its failure leads to intracellular Na⁺ accumulation and K⁺ loss.

Internal Na⁺ and Cl⁻: Na⁺ concentration rises inside the cell because it cannot be pumped out and continues to leak in. Cl⁻ also enters the cell, following the electrical gradient created by the retained Na⁺ and the Donnan effect (negatively charged proteins attract cations, which in turn attract Cl⁻).

Structural integrity: The increased intracellular osmolarity (due to Na⁺, Cl⁻, and proteins) causes water to move into the cell. The cell swells, and if the swelling is severe, the plasma membrane may rupture (lysis), leading to cell death. This is a key mechanism of ischemic cell injury.
