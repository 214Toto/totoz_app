Question 1
State 3 physiological reasons why infants and children are more vulnerable to develop dehydration than adults [1.5 marks]
1. Higher surface area to volume ratio: Infants have a larger body surface area relative to their volume, leading to greater insensible water loss through the skin and lungs. This is a major reason for rapid dehydration.
2. Higher metabolic rate: Infants have a higher metabolic rate per kilogram of body weight, which increases heat production and thus evaporative water loss. They also turn over water more rapidly than adults.
3. Immature kidneys: The kidneys of infants are less developed and have a reduced ability to concentrate urine (lower renal concentrating capacity), meaning they excrete more water to eliminate solutes. This predisposes them to dehydration, especially during diarrhoeal illness.
Other accepted answers: Higher ECF-to-ICF ratio, inability to independently access water, and higher respiratory rate.
Question 2
State 3 clinical importance of determining the hematocrit [1.5 marks]
1. Diagnosis and classification of anaemia: Haematocrit (packed cell volume) is a key component in calculating red cell indices (MCV, MCH, MCHC), which help classify anaemias as microcytic, normocytic, or macrocytic.
2. Estimation of blood volume and detection of polycythaemia: A raised haematocrit may indicate polycythaemia (primary or secondary), while a low haematocrit suggests anaemia or haemodilution. It also helps estimate total blood volume in surgical and trauma settings.
3. Monitoring hydration status and response to therapy: Haematocrit is used to assess dehydration (elevated haematocrit due to haemoconcentration) and to monitor fluid resuscitation. It also aids in detecting bleeding and guiding transfusion decisions.
Other accepted answers: Assessment of oxygen-carrying capacity and monitoring in chronic hypoxia.
Question 3
State and explain 3 consequences of hypoalbuminemia [1.5 marks]
1. Oedema (anasarca in severe cases): Albumin is the major contributor to plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure. A decrease in albumin reduces the osmotic force that normally draws fluid back into the capillaries from the interstitial space. This results in fluid accumulation in tissues (oedema), particularly in dependent areas and the peritoneal cavity (ascites).
2. Reduced drug binding and altered pharmacokinetics: Many drugs (e.g., warfarin, phenytoin, salicylates) are bound to albumin in the plasma. Hypoalbuminaemia reduces the amount of binding protein, increasing the free (active) fraction of these drugs and potentially causing toxicity even at standard doses.
3. Nephrotic syndrome and hyperlipidaemia: In nephrotic syndrome, hypoalbuminaemia occurs due to renal loss of protein. The liver compensates by increasing synthesis of lipoproteins, leading to hyperlipidaemia. It also causes a hypercoagulable state due to loss of antithrombin III.
Other accepted answers: Impaired transport of hormones, fatty acids, and bilirubin.
Question 4
State and explain the 3 steps in the formation of the primary platelet plug formation, including the substances that mediate them [1.5 marks]
1. Platelet adhesion: When the endothelium is damaged, platelets adhere to exposed subendothelial collagen. This is mediated by von Willebrand factor (vWF), which binds both collagen and platelet glycoprotein Ib (GpIb). Without vWF, adhesion is impaired (as in von Willebrand disease).
2. Platelet activation: Adhesion triggers platelet activation, leading to a shape change (from discoid to spiky), release of granule contents (ADP, serotonin, thromboxane A₂, platelet factor 3), and expression of glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptors. Thromboxane A₂ and ADP are key mediators that recruit more platelets and promote aggregation.
3. Platelet aggregation: Activated platelets aggregate via fibrinogen bridges binding to GpIIb/IIIa receptors on adjacent platelets. This forms the primary haemostatic plug. Thrombin also promotes aggregation and stabilises the plug by converting fibrinogen to fibrin.
Note: Vasoconstriction is a preceding step but is not part of the platelet plug itself.
Question 5
State and explain 2 sites where intravascular clotting may occur in the body [1 mark]
1. Deep veins of the legs (venous thromboembolism): Intravascular clotting commonly occurs in the deep veins of the lower limbs, especially after prolonged immobility (e.g., surgery, long flights). Stasis of blood, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability (Virchow's triad) lead to thrombus formation. This can lead to pulmonary embolism if the clot dislodges.
2. Arteries (arterial thrombosis): Clotting can occur in arteries, particularly at sites of atherosclerotic plaque rupture. Platelet adhesion and activation are triggered by exposed collagen and tissue factor, leading to thrombus formation. This can cause myocardial infarction or stroke.
Other accepted answers: Microvasculature (DIC), intracardiac (mural thrombi).
Question 6
State and explain 2 dangers of hemolysis [1 mark]
1. Haemolytic anaemia: Excessive destruction of red blood cells reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, leading to anaemia, fatigue, dyspnoea, and tachycardia. If the bone marrow cannot compensate, the anaemia becomes severe.
2. Jaundice and kernicterus: Haemolysis releases haemoglobin, which is broken down into bilirubin. Excess bilirubin causes jaundice (yellow discolouration of skin and sclera). In newborns, unconjugated bilirubin can cross the immature blood-brain barrier and deposit in the basal ganglia, causing kernicterus (a form of brain damage).
Other accepted answers: Pigment gallstones, iron overload, and renal failure due to haemoglobinuria.
Question 7
State and mention the functions of the subpopulation of T lymphocytes [1 mark]
1. Helper T cells (CD4⁺): Recognise antigen presented by MHC class II molecules. They secrete cytokines (e.g., IL-2, IFN-γ) that activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells, and macrophages, orchestrating both humoral and cell-mediated immunity.
2. Cytotoxic T cells (CD8⁺): Recognise antigen presented by MHC class I molecules. They directly kill infected host cells, tumour cells, and foreign graft cells by releasing perforin and granzymes.
3. Regulatory T cells (Tregs): Suppress immune responses and prevent autoimmunity by inhibiting the activity of other T cells.
Other accepted answers: Memory T cells, which provide long-term immunity.
Question 8
State four mechanisms by which antibodies directly attack the invading micro-organism [1 mark]
1. Neutralisation: Antibodies bind to toxins or viral surface proteins, preventing them from interacting with host cell receptors and causing damage or infection.
2. Opsonisation: Antibodies coat the surface of pathogens, marking them for phagocytosis by macrophages and neutrophils, which have Fc receptors.
3. Complement activation: IgM and IgG antibodies activate the classical complement pathway, leading to the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) that lyses the pathogen, and to opsonisation via C3b.
4. Agglutination: Antibodies cross-link pathogens, clumping them together and reducing their ability to spread and infect. This enhances phagocytosis.
Other accepted answers: Antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC).
Question 9
State one condition that may cause an increase in afterload. Also state and explain 3 consequences of afterload. [2 marks]
Condition: Hypertension (or aortic stenosis, atherosclerosis).

Consequences of increased afterload:
1. Decreased stroke volume: Increased afterload means the ventricle must generate higher pressure to eject blood. If the ventricle cannot overcome this, stroke volume decreases, reducing cardiac output.
2. Increased myocardial oxygen demand: The heart must work harder to pump against higher resistance, increasing oxygen consumption. This can precipitate ischaemia in patients with coronary artery disease.
3. Ventricular hypertrophy: Chronic pressure overload leads to concentric hypertrophy of the left ventricle, which initially compensates but eventually can lead to diastolic dysfunction and heart failure.
Other accepted answers: Reduced ejection fraction, increased wall stress (Laplace's law).
Question 10
Explain 2 importance of the elasticity (compliance) of the aorta [1 mark]
1. Damping of pressure oscillations (Windkessel effect): The elastic aorta expands during systole to accommodate the stroke volume, storing potential energy. During diastole, it recoils, maintaining diastolic pressure and ensuring continuous perfusion of tissues (especially the coronary arteries) throughout the cardiac cycle. Loss of elasticity (arteriosclerosis) leads to isolated systolic hypertension and reduced coronary perfusion.
2. Reduction of cardiac workload: Elasticity reduces the peak pressure the left ventricle must generate during systole, lowering myocardial oxygen demand. It also prevents excessive pressure fluctuations from damaging smaller vessels.
Other accepted answers: Maintenance of diastolic pressure and prevention of aortic damage.
Question 11
Briefly describe the vagal escape. [1.5 mark]
Vagal escape is the phenomenon in which prolonged, intense vagal (parasympathetic) stimulation to the heart initially causes marked bradycardia or even transient asystole, but the heart eventually resumes beating at a faster rate despite continued vagal stimulation.

Mechanism: This occurs because the SA node and the Purkinje fibres can develop intrinsic rhythms independent of vagal tone. The sympathetic nervous system may also be reflexively activated, and the pacemaker cells in the AV node and ventricles can take over as secondary pacemakers. This prevents prolonged cardiac arrest.
Significance: It protects against excessive vagal-induced cardiac standstill.
Question 12
Mention one benefit of the vagal escape [1 mark]
It prevents prolonged cardiac arrest (asystole) and maintains cardiac output and tissue perfusion during intense vagal stimulation. It acts as a safety mechanism against excessive parasympathetic inhibition of the heart.
Question 13
Explain the relationship between cardiac output, stroke volume, and heart rate in responding to hemorrhage. [2.5 marks]
Cardiac output (CO) = Stroke volume (SV) × Heart rate (HR).

In haemorrhage:
1. Decreased stroke volume: Loss of blood reduces venous return and ventricular filling (preload). According to the Frank-Starling mechanism, reduced preload decreases stroke volume.
2. Compensatory tachycardia: The baroreceptor reflex triggers sympathetic activation, which increases heart rate (tachycardia) in an attempt to maintain cardiac output. Initially, this compensates for the reduced stroke volume.
3. Decreased cardiac output: If haemorrhage is severe, the compensatory increase in heart rate is insufficient to maintain cardiac output, and CO falls. This leads to hypotension and reduced tissue perfusion. Eventually, if not corrected, decompensation and shock occur.
Summary: Haemorrhage → ↓ preload → ↓ SV → ↑ HR (compensatory) → CO initially maintained, but eventually ↓.

Scenario 1 – Myocardial Infarction with Complete Heart Block
A 68-year-old woman presents to the emergency department of the Ndola Teaching Hospital with shortness of breath, light-headedness, and chest pain described as being like "an elephant sitting on her chest." She is diagnosed with a myocardial infarction. She is given oxygen and an aspirin to chew and is not felt to be a candidate for thrombolytic therapy. Her heart rate is 40 beats per minute (bpm). Although there are P waves, they seem to be dissociated from the QRS complexes on the electrocardiograph (ECG). The patient is diagnosed with complete heart block, probably as a result of her myocardial infarction.
a) Describe the role of aspirin in the acute management of a patient with suspected MI and how it works at the physiological level. [2 marks]
Aspirin is given acutely in suspected myocardial infarction because it is a rapid-acting antiplatelet agent. It irreversibly inhibits the enzyme cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) in platelets, preventing the synthesis of thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂) from arachidonic acid. TXA₂ is a potent platelet aggregator and vasoconstrictor. By blocking its production, aspirin reduces platelet aggregation, limits thrombus growth, and helps prevent further coronary occlusion. This reduces myocardial damage and mortality. Because platelets lack a nucleus, the effect lasts for the lifespan of the platelet (7–10 days).
b) How does heart rate affect cardiac output, and why might a heart rate of 40 bpm be concerning for this patient with a myocardial infarction? [2 marks]
Cardiac output (CO) is the product of heart rate (HR) and stroke volume (SV). Within physiological limits, an increase in HR increases CO. However, at very high rates, diastolic filling time is reduced, decreasing SV and eventually CO. Conversely, bradycardia (HR < 60 bpm) can also reduce CO if the stroke volume cannot increase enough to compensate. In this patient, a HR of 40 bpm (bradycardia due to complete heart block) is concerning because: (1) It reduces cardiac output, leading to hypotension, light-headedness, and reduced tissue perfusion, especially to the already ischaemic myocardium. (2) It can worsen myocardial ischaemia by reducing coronary perfusion pressure and oxygen delivery. (3) It may progress to asystole or heart failure.
c) What is the role of the AV node in the normal cardiac conduction system, and why is it significant in this clinical scenario? [2 marks]
The AV node is responsible for conducting electrical impulses from the atria to the ventricles. It introduces a delay of about 0.1 seconds, allowing the atria to contract and fill the ventricles before ventricular contraction. It also acts as a secondary pacemaker (40–60 bpm) if the SA node fails. In this patient, the AV node is likely damaged by the myocardial infarction (inferior MI often supplies the AV node via the right coronary artery). This damage has caused complete heart block, where atrial impulses (P waves) are not conducted to the ventricles. The ventricles are driven by a slower, escape rhythm originating below the block (e.g., bundle of His or Purkinje fibres), resulting in bradycardia.
d) Clinically, when an electrocardiogram (ECG) shows a dissociation between the P wave and the QRS complex, which structures within the heart take over as the primary pacemaker, and what type of rhythm does this usually signify? [2 marks]
When there is complete dissociation between P waves and QRS complexes (complete heart block), the ventricles are driven by an escape pacemaker distal to the block. This is usually the AV junction (AV node or bundle of His) or the Purkinje fibres in the ventricles. If the block is at the AV node, a junctional escape rhythm occurs at 40–60 bpm. If the block is below the AV node (infranodal), a ventricular escape rhythm occurs at 20–40 bpm. This signifies third-degree (complete) heart block, a serious condition that often requires a pacemaker.
Scenario 2 – Prolonged Prothrombin Time after Gastrectomy
A 42-year-old female presents to Kitwe Teaching Hospital with the chief complaint of frequent diarrhea accompanied by weight loss, following total gastrectomy. He reports a tendency to bruise easily and laboratory data reveal a prothrombin time of 19 seconds (normal = 11–14 seconds).
a) What symptoms and signs might be associated with the prolonged prothrombin time in this patient? [1 mark]
Easy bruising, bleeding gums, epistaxis (nosebleeds), prolonged bleeding from minor cuts, haematuria, and in severe cases, gastrointestinal bleeding or intracranial haemorrhage. The patient already reports easy bruising.
b) Which component of the blood clotting cascade is assessed by the prothrombin time (PT) test? [1 mark]
The prothrombin time (PT) assesses the extrinsic pathway and the common pathway. It is sensitive to deficiencies of factors I (fibrinogen), II (prothrombin), V, VII, and X. The most important factor assessed is factor VII (which has the shortest half-life).
c) What is a potential cause of the prolonged prothrombin time in this patient with diarrhea and weight loss? [1 mark]
Vitamin K deficiency. This is likely due to malabsorption following total gastrectomy and chronic diarrhoea. Vitamin K is a fat-soluble vitamin absorbed in the ileum; fat malabsorption leads to deficiency, which impairs the synthesis of functional factors II, VII, IX, and X.
d) Which specific dietary component is essential for the absorption of vitamin K? [1 mark]
Dietary fat (and bile salts). Vitamin K is fat-soluble and requires bile and pancreatic lipase for absorption. In this patient, malabsorption of fat leads to impaired vitamin K absorption.
e) How does vitamin K deficiency affect blood clotting? [1 mark]
Vitamin K is a cofactor for the gamma-carboxylation of glutamate residues on factors II, VII, IX, and X. This carboxylation is essential for these factors to bind calcium and phospholipids on activated platelets. Without vitamin K, these factors are synthesized but remain non-functional, leading to prolonged PT and a bleeding tendency.
f) What other clotting tests might be useful in evaluating the patient's coagulation status? [1 mark]
Bleeding Time and Clotting Time.
g) How can a vitamin K deficiency be treated in this particular patient? [1 mark]
Vitamin K supplementation. This can be given orally (phytonadione) or parenterally (intramuscular or intravenous). In severe bleeding, fresh frozen plasma (FFP) or prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) may be given to rapidly replace the deficient factors. Dietary advice to increase vitamin K-rich foods (green leafy vegetables) is also helpful.
h) Why would a liver function test be a necessary assessment in this patient? [1 mark]
Because all the vitamin K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X) are synthesised in the liver. If the liver is damaged (e.g., due to malnutrition, metastasis, or other disease), it cannot produce these factors even in the presence of vitamin K. Liver function tests (e.g., ALT, AST, ALP, bilirubin, albumin) help determine whether the prolonged PT is due to vitamin K deficiency alone or to underlying liver dysfunction.
