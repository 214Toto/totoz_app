1 Fluid Mosaic Model

Describe the structural organization of the cell membrane according to the Fluid Mosaic Model, detailing the roles of phospholipids, integral/peripheral proteins, and cholesterol in maintaining membrane function and fluidity.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Fluid Mosaic Model: The membrane is a fluid phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins.

  • Phospholipids: Amphipathic — polar heads face aqueous environments, nonpolar tails face inward. Form the basic barrier.
  • Integral proteins: Embedded in the bilayer; act as channels, carriers, pumps, receptors.
  • Peripheral proteins: Attached to membrane surface (e.g., ankyrin, spectrin); involved in cytoskeletal anchoring and signaling.
  • Cholesterol: Inserts between phospholipids; at high temperatures decreases fluidity, at low temperatures prevents tight packing — maintains fluidity across temperature ranges.

2 Fick's Law & Factors

State Fick's Law of Diffusion. List and explain the six major factors that influence the net rate of diffusion across a biological membrane.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Fick's Law: Q = (P · A · ΔC) / ΔX, where Q = net rate of diffusion, P = permeability, A = surface area, ΔC = concentration gradient, ΔX = membrane thickness.

Six factors:

  • Concentration gradient (ΔC): Directly proportional — steeper gradient → faster diffusion.
  • Membrane surface area (A): Directly proportional — larger area → faster diffusion.
  • Membrane thickness (ΔX): Inversely proportional — thicker membrane → slower diffusion.
  • Permeability (P): Depends on lipid solubility, size, and charge — directly proportional.
  • Temperature: Higher temperature → increased kinetic energy → faster diffusion.
  • Molecular size: Smaller molecules diffuse faster.

3 Simple vs Facilitated Diffusion

Compare and contrast Simple Diffusion and Facilitated Diffusion in terms of carrier requirement, energy consumption, transport kinetics (Vmax), and typical transported substrates.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

  • Carrier requirement: Simple — no carrier. Facilitated — requires carrier protein.
  • Energy: Both are passive (no ATP).
  • Kinetics: Simple — linear (rate ∝ ΔC). Facilitated — saturable, follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics, reaches Vmax.
  • Substrates: Simple — small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, steroids). Facilitated — polar molecules (glucose, amino acids, ions) that cannot cross the lipid bilayer directly.

4 Osmosis & Tonicity

Define Osmosis and Osmotic Pressure. Differentiate between isotonic, hypotonic, and hypertonic solutions, explaining their physiological effects on human red blood cells.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Osmosis: Net movement of water across a semipermeable membrane from low solute concentration to high solute concentration.

Osmotic pressure: The pressure required to prevent osmotic water movement.

  • Isotonic (e.g., 0.9% NaCl): Same osmolarity as plasma — RBCs maintain normal shape.
  • Hypotonic: Lower osmolarity — water enters RBCs → swelling → hemolysis.
  • Hypertonic: Higher osmolarity — water leaves RBCs → shrinkage (crenation).

5 Nernst Equation

Write down the Nernst Equation and explain its physiological relevance. State the equilibrium potentials calculated for Na⁺, K⁺, and Cl⁻ ions.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Nernst Equation: Eion = (RT/zF) · ln([ion]out/[ion]in) = (61/z) · log([ion]out/[ion]in) at 37°C.

Physiological relevance: Predicts the membrane potential at which there is no net ion flow. Helps understand resting membrane potential and action potentials.

Equilibrium potentials: ENa ≈ +61 mV; EK ≈ −94 mV; ECl ≈ −70 mV.

6 Starling Forces

Explain the capillary filtration process using Starling forces. Calculate the Net Filtration Pressure given: Capillary Hydrostatic Pressure = 28 mmHg, Interstitial Hydrostatic Pressure = −2 mmHg, and Colloidal Osmotic Pressure = 25 mmHg.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Starling forces: (1) Capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc) — pushes fluid out. (2) Interstitial hydrostatic pressure (Pi) — opposes filtration. (3) Colloidal osmotic pressure (πc) — pulls fluid in. (4) Interstitial osmotic pressure (πi) — usually negligible.

Calculation: NFP = (Pc − Pi) − πc = (28 − (−2)) − 25 = 30 − 25 = 5 mmHg. Net filtration occurs.

7 Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase Pump

Elucidate the operational mechanism of the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase pump. Describe its stoichiometry, electrogenic nature, physiological functions, and regulatory factors (hormones and drugs).

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Mechanism: For each ATP hydrolyzed, 3 Na⁺ are pumped out and 2 K⁺ are pumped in, against their concentration gradients (primary active transport).

Electrogenic: Because 3 Na⁺ out vs 2 K⁺ in, the pump is electrogenic — contributes to negative resting membrane potential.

Functions: Maintains Na⁺/K⁺ gradients, regulates cell volume, provides energy for secondary active transport (e.g., glucose, amino acids), maintains resting potential.

Regulation: Stimulated by thyroid hormone, aldosterone, insulin. Inhibited by dopamine, digitalis, ouabain.

8 Transepithelial Glucose Transport

Describe the transepithelial transport of glucose across renal tubular epithelium (or intestinal mucosa), detailing the roles of SGLT-1 on the luminal side, the Na⁺-K⁺ pump on the basal side, and GLUT-2 on the basolateral membrane.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Luminal side: SGLT-1 (Na⁺-glucose co-transporter) uses the Na⁺ gradient to move glucose against its concentration gradient into the cell (secondary active transport).

Basolateral side: Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase pumps Na⁺ out, maintaining the low intracellular Na⁺ concentration needed for SGLT-1. GLUT-2 facilitates glucose exit into the blood (facilitated diffusion).

Net result: Glucose is absorbed from lumen → cell → blood.

9 Carrier Classification

Classify carrier-mediated transporters into Uniporters, Symporters, and Antiporters, providing ONE distinct physiological example for each class.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

  • Uniporter: Transports one solute. Example: GLUT-1 (glucose transporter).
  • Symporter: Transports two solutes in the same direction. Example: SGLT-1 (Na⁺-glucose co-transporter).
  • Antiporter: Transports two solutes in opposite directions. Example: Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCX).

10 Endocytosis Types

Compare and contrast the three major types of endocytosis: Pinocytosis, Receptor-Mediated Endocytosis, and Phagocytosis.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

  • Pinocytosis: "Cell drinking" — non-specific uptake of extracellular fluid and small solutes. Uses membrane-deforming coat proteins and dynamin.
  • Receptor-Mediated Endocytosis: Specific uptake of molecules (e.g., LDL, viruses) via receptors. Uses clathrin-coated pits, actin, and myosin.
  • Phagocytosis: "Cell eating" — engulfment of large particles (bacteria, debris) by specialized cells (macrophages, neutrophils). Requires actin polymerization and pseudopod formation.

1 Digoxin & Cardiac Contractility

A 58-year-old patient with chronic heart failure is prescribed Digoxin (a cardiac glycoside). Explain the cellular mechanism of action of Digoxin on the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase pump and describe how this alters the secondary active Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger to increase intracellular Ca²⁺ and enhance cardiac contractility.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Mechanism: Digoxin inhibits the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase pump → intracellular Na⁺ increases. The Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCX) normally uses the Na⁺ gradient to extrude Ca²⁺. With elevated intracellular Na⁺, the exchanger works less efficiently (or reverses), leading to increased intracellular Ca²⁺. More Ca²⁺ available for cardiac muscle contraction → increased contractility (positive inotropy).

2 Oral Rehydration Therapy

A 6-year-old child presents with severe dehydration and watery diarrhea due to a gastrointestinal infection. The attending physician prescribes Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) containing a precise mixture of sodium and glucose. Explain the physiological transport mechanism in the intestinal mucosa that makes the combination of sodium and glucose effective for water reabsorption.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Mechanism: SGLT-1 co-transports Na⁺ and glucose into intestinal cells. Na⁺ is then pumped out by Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase, creating an osmotic gradient that draws water into the cells and then into the blood. Glucose enhances Na⁺ absorption, which in turn drives water absorption. This is why ORT with both Na⁺ and glucose is more effective than either alone.

3 Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia

A 24-year-old athlete undergoes an endurance test in extreme heat and drinks massive amounts of pure distilled water, resulting in exercise-associated hyponatremia. ABG and plasma analysis show a significantly reduced plasma osmolarity (240 mOsm/L). Explain the osmotic shift of water across cell membranes and predict the structural fate of the patient's red blood cells.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Osmotic shift: Plasma osmolarity (240 mOsm/L) is lower than intracellular osmolarity (≈300 mOsm/L). Water moves by osmosis into cells, causing them to swell.

RBC fate: Red blood cells swell and may undergo hemolysis (rupture) if the hypotonic stress is severe. This can lead to anemia and release of hemoglobin into plasma.

4 Dynamin & Clathrin Inhibition

A researcher tests a new investigational drug that selectively inhibits the enzyme Dynamin and destroys Clathrin coat proteins in human cell cultures. Describe the specific vesicular transport processes that will be disrupted in these cells, giving examples of specific molecules or particles that will fail to enter the cells.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Disrupted processes: Receptor-mediated endocytosis and pinocytosis (both require clathrin and dynamin for vesicle formation and scission).

Examples of molecules that fail to enter: LDL (low-density lipoprotein), transferrin, viruses (e.g., influenza, HIV), and other ligands taken up by coated pits. Fluid-phase pinocytosis is also affected.

5 Channelopathy

A geneticist identifies a point mutation in a patient presenting with episodic muscle weakness and cardiac arrhythmias. Further molecular testing confirms a channelopathy involving voltage-gated sodium channels. Differentiate between leaky channels, voltage-gated channels, and ligand-gated channels, and explain how a channelopathy disrupts normal cellular membrane potentials.

Click to see model answer

Model Answer

Leaky channels: Always open, contribute to resting membrane potential (e.g., K⁺ leak channels).

Voltage-gated channels: Open in response to changes in membrane potential (e.g., Na⁺, K⁺ channels in action potentials).

Ligand-gated channels: Open when a specific neurotransmitter binds (e.g., ACh receptor at neuromuscular junction).

Channelopathy: A mutation in a channel gene alters its function (e.g., prolonged Na⁺ channel opening). This disrupts normal action potential generation and propagation, leading to hyperexcitability or inexcitability — causing muscle weakness, arrhythmias, or periodic paralysis.
